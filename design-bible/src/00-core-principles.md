# 00. Core principles

This chapter is the short version of the book. It sets out forty principles for designing a roguelike, the twelve disagreements a designer most needs to settle for their own game, a brief to work through when starting a new design, and suggested routes through the eighteen chapters that follow. Everything here is developed at length elsewhere in the bible, and each principle points to where.

The bible is a synthesis of what practitioners said in 311 talks given at Roguelike Celebration between 2016 and 2026: developers of shipped games, toolmakers, researchers, artists and players. It is not a proof. A principle appears here because several of those people arrived at it independently, usually in different years and about different games, or because it is the idea a whole chapter rests on. Where the support comes from fewer voices, the text says so. The principles are written as imperatives because that is how a designer uses them, but each one is a summary of what speakers reported working, and every cited statement links to the moment in a talk where someone said it.

Use the chapter in three ways. Read the principles as a checklist against a design you already have, and argue with the ones that do not fit; they are meant to be specific enough to disagree with. Read the fault lines before you commit to a direction, because they mark the places where credible designers chose differently and the choice depends on your game. And use the design brief at the start of a project, when the questions are cheapest to answer.

## The principles

The principles run from the inside out: what a run is for, how death and chance shape it, how the player learns it, the systems they act through, the generators, the story, and the interface, tools and process.

### What a run is for

**1. Decide whether your game is about mastering a challenge or inhabiting a world, and make every system answer to that choice.**
Zeno Rogue names the two approaches, mastery of a challenge and immersion in a complex simulated world, and designs HyperRogue for mastery {{wOlSws-OI2o#5}}; Max Kreminski values roguelikes for trying to be worlds that exist independently of the player {{TvlZinAvpwg#1}}. George Moromisato sets NetHack's mastery and variety against a AAA game's one-time experience and production quality {{QBZkvtGucBU#0}}, and in Q&A suggested, without being certain, that these contrasts are sliders a designer balances rather than a binary {{QBZkvtGucBU#14}}. Tarn Adams adds why the choice matters: even "replayability" means different things (mastering interactions, being surprised, inhabiting a world) depending on which flavour of roguelike you make {{4-7TtPX5uhg#2}}. See [01. What is a roguelike?](01-what-is-a-roguelike.md).

**2. Treat permadeath, procedural generation, consumables and turns as one package: for every part you drop, decide what now does its job.**
bhauth describes the parts as leaning on each other: permadeath forces players to replay the start, which needs procedural generation for variety, whose uneven difficulty needs consumables, which need turns in which to use them {{tg-CjNodlmY#6}}. Max Kreminski, following Kate Compton, defines a genre the same way, as mutually supportive patterns that shore up each other's weaknesses, so that adopting a few pulls a design toward the rest {{TvlZinAvpwg#3}} {{BDcZSWHu8RQ#15}}. Several of these parts began as answers to technical limits rather than as doctrine: Aaron A. Reed notes that early games generated worlds because a hand-made one would not fit in memory {{CE7o3GGif30#0}}, and the Rise of the Roguelite panel's moderator traces permadeath and level randomness to constraints that later games feel obliged to justify in fiction {{ujKlmvfpNMo#11}}. So keep each part for what it does in your game now, not because the genre always had it; Eiríkr Åsheim, for one, values Rogue's use of procedural generation to minimise assets for the permacomputing values it serves today {{qogCwa12jEg#2}}. See [01. What is a roguelike?](01-what-is-a-roguelike.md) and [02. History and lineage: where the conventions came from](02-history-and-lineage.md).

**3. When you take roguelike structure somewhere new, name the element you are borrowing and adapt it to its new home rather than copying the surface.**
Shawn Main reduces the roguelike to a framework other genres can use: mutually exclusive choices that grow the player's power, varied and escalating challenges that test them, and a run that ends {{WLBJJTc6LWI#0}} {{WLBJJTc6LWI#1}}. The successful transplants in the talks each picked one element on purpose: Against the Storm distilled a city builder's first hours into a run {{tWFIQaeSs6M#0}}, and Legends of Runeterra used runs to restore the power progression its generous card collection had removed {{WLBJJTc6LWI#2}}. Gillian Smith describes a university that keeps runs but drops permanent failure so students take risks, and contrasts that with points-and-badges gamification, "chocolate-covered broccoli" {{R8DSq8qSJ44@3555#0}} {{R8DSq8qSJ44@3555#1}}. Tyriq Plummer shows two platformers borrowing nearly the same roguelike mechanics and feeling completely different, because which mechanics you borrow and how you apply them matters more than the proportion {{OVSi3NzA0KM#7}}. See [18. Beyond roguelikes](18-beyond-roguelikes.md).

### Death, difficulty and randomness

**4. Keep permadeath for what it does to decisions, and make sure your game has choices that matter only because they cannot be undone.**
On the Rogue panel, Glenn Wichman proposed calling permadeath "consequence persistence": if players could save, drink an unknown potion and restore, the whole mystery-item mechanic would disappear {{IaB7iQhts_c#15}}, and the panel added that good outcomes are as permanent as bad ones {{IaB7iQhts_c#16}}. bhauth argues permadeath makes players weigh probabilities across a huge space of outcomes, and compares a roguelike without it to poker with save and reload {{tg-CjNodlmY#7}}. Sersa Victory uses the same irreversibility against consumable hoarding, since a run cannot be retried {{TeURjrAYFEU#17}}. If nothing in your design depends on irreversibility, permadeath is only a penalty. See [03. Death, difficulty and progression](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md).

**5. Make every death legible: the player should be able to say what killed them and what they would do differently.**
George Moromisato credits NetHack's "stupid deaths" with sustaining play because the player always knows exactly why they died and believes they could have avoided it {{QBZkvtGucBU#3}}. Andrew Aversa defines good roguelike difficulty as challenges that are understandable, with the player feeling responsible for their death {{Gluz5nIplcA#0}}; Alan Malloy calls a fun death one that carries a tactical lesson, not one avoidable only by grinding {{QV57CYrIZow#13}}; and Gillian Smith says failure is fun only when it is normalised, with players able to name what they carry away from it {{R8DSq8qSJ44@3555#3}}. Dungeon Crawl Stone Soup's morgue files show how: they record the messages before death and a snapshot of the map, so players can reconstruct who dealt damage and from where {{a9flzzXJVcg#1}}. Jeff Lait shows the cost of failing at this: players who fail at a long-term loop often do not recognise it and simply call the game bad {{LQPGmxytTws#3}}. See [03. Death, difficulty and progression](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md) and [04. Learning and discovery](04-learning-and-discovery.md).

**6. Get the player from death to the next real decision in seconds, and spend your variety on the opening that every run replays.**
John Harris puts it simply: the longer a roguelike takes to start playing, the worse it is {{b45orGdFwi0#8}}. Lisa Brown found Nuclear Throne, which gets you back into play in about five seconds, stickier than Enter the Gungeon with its longer death sequence {{BjqZdMW8U9Q#3}}. Because default permadeath promises that early content will be seen again and again, Andrew Aversa argues for investing heavily in early-game variety {{kn2QkH5lByw#4}}, and Shawn Main for distinct enforced starting positions such as NetHack's classes or Hades' first-room boon {{WLBJJTc6LWI#12}}. The same arithmetic rules out long story at the start: Tim Keenan calls intro cutscenes and story that replays every run a burden {{G5rq5ei2Lec#2}}. See [03. Death, difficulty and progression](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md).

**7. Carry knowledge, options and story across runs before you carry raw power.**
Several talks put the player's knowledge at the heart of the genre: it is what survives permadeath, and learning across runs is the progression a roguelike really offers {{WtdCY6NxX6I#0}} {{FZn4NeQOcFE#2}} {{SDojcmObQzw#9}}, and Arvi Teikari describes Noita relying on that knowledge instead of unlocking more powerful items {{huMHQGr8PgM#15}}. Shawn Main argues that players want their time rewarded {{WLBJJTc6LWI#15}}, but Jim Shepard shows what meta power costs: too much makes the early game a walkover, and tuning late content for players who have died many times builds a grind wall for first-run players {{FZn4NeQOcFE#7}}. The safer forms in the talks speed up the early game instead of raising the ceiling {{Gluz5nIplcA#5}}, unlock options that widen future choices {{FZn4NeQOcFE#4}}, let the world respond to past runs without changing difficulty {{FZn4NeQOcFE#8}}, or, in Nic Junius's braided narratives, advance someone's story on almost every run {{sOZH6RPuNJ8#9}}. See [03. Death, difficulty and progression](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md) and [12. Narrative and worldbuilding](12-narrative-and-worldbuilding.md).

**8. Let randomness set the problem rather than decide the outcome, and trim the extremes that a short run cannot average out.**
Shawn Main separates setup randomness, met before the decision and experienced as novelty to adapt to, from results randomness, where a roll decides the outcome and players feel they lost to the dice {{UZ7XqQYDM7o#3}} {{UZ7XqQYDM7o#4}}. Subset Games describe Into the Breach as moving randomness to the input, with random spawns but deterministic player actions {{rNi5nO5yqLs#9}}, and David Dunham moved Thousand Hells from opaque dice to a visible shuffled deck {{oipNr8RE5bA#1}}. Evan Debenham explains why randomness still needs shaping: a single run is too short for outcomes to converge, so smooth the extremes while keeping the game exciting {{kaojiu9syPE#5}} {{kaojiu9syPE#1}}; Sébastien Benard starts from a balanced, non-random allocation and adds chaos back on purpose {{QbTnKvRrNYY#13}}, and Brogue paces its reward rooms on a rubber band {{Uo9-IcHhq_w#7}}. Main also marks the limit: smooth away all the variance and the game becomes flat micro-optimisation {{UZ7XqQYDM7o#14}}. See [03. Death, difficulty and progression](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md) and [11. Generative methods](11-generative-methods.md).

**9. Price every action, especially waiting.**
Nicholas Feinberg's test is to imagine a player with infinite patience and ask whether their win-maximising use of a system is fun {{b8uoQHedFV0#0}}; if it is not, the design suffers even for players who never do it, because after each death they suspect they lost by skipping the grind {{b8uoQHedFV0#3}}. Feinberg traces the problem to actions that cost nothing, above all nearly free out-of-combat turns {{b8uoQHedFV0#7}} {{b8uoQHedFV0#10}}. The evidence comes from several directions: hunger exists to turn time into a resource weighed against other options {{j9ykOdQRUQk#0}} {{WLBJJTc6LWI#13}}; Tangledeep became trivially easy when its hunger clock was removed while health still regenerated, because players could kite and wait {{Gluz5nIplcA#7}}; and Angband's players turned out to win more games per hour by diving than by the cautious play the community long thought optimal {{LaOcggk7U88#2}}. Dylan White adds the opposite failure: time is a resource in itself, and in Rift Wizard players left spells unused because they did not feel worth a turn {{kvgKKRLil_8#9}}. See [03. Death, difficulty and progression](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md), [05. Systems and emergence](05-systems-and-emergence.md) and [06. Combat and tactics](06-combat-and-tactics.md).

**10. Offer difficulty as granular, clearly labelled options, and design the softer modes rather than just switching death off.**
Andrea Roberts argues that because difficulty is subjective, players need tools to tune it themselves {{WtdCY6NxX6I#9}}, and Philomena Schwab that different players find different parts hard, so separate settings serve more of them than one slider {{wLLDPz8PnQg#2}}. Dan DiIorio answered conflicting "too easy" and "too hard" feedback with twenty unlockable difficulties rather than a new baseline {{fSmaqjbU2xg#0}}, and Roundguard learned to label its easy mode clearly after streamers equipped its mercy relic without realising what it was {{WtdCY6NxX6I#13}}. Andrew Aversa treats permadeath itself as a spectrum {{kn2QkH5lByw#0}}: about 30% of Tangledeep's players chose its non-permadeath mode {{Gluz5nIplcA#6}}, but hazards acceptable without permadeath feel unfair with it, so both modes need design work {{kn2QkH5lByw#7}}, and the default mode shapes how players perceive the whole game {{kn2QkH5lByw#1}}. See [03. Death, difficulty and progression](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md).

### Learning and legibility

**11. Teach principles, not routes: write down what separates an expert from a novice, and make sure the game itself can teach it.**
bhauth proposes judging a game by the difference between what new and very good players do, because that difference is what the game teaches {{tg-CjNodlmY#4}}. Brian Walker and Herbert Wolverson independently credit random levels with moving the skill from memorising routes to mastering systems {{Uo9-IcHhq_w#0}} {{TlLIOgWYVpI#0}}, Andrea Roberts contrasts rehearsing a Mario level with Spelunky's need for principles and improvisation {{2BHfpAF76J8#5}}, and Pandamander describes roguelikes pushing players "out of book" into situations where they must generalise {{KtQh4QrsUzs#6}}. Gillian Smith asks designers to decide what players should learn {{R8DSq8qSJ44@3555#13}}, warning from teaching that the lesson actually learned is rarely the intended one {{R8DSq8qSJ44@3555#9}}. Teaching should not interfere with that learning: Roberts limits Roundguard's tutorial to foundations {{WtdCY6NxX6I#1}} {{WtdCY6NxX6I#4}}, and Luck be a Landlord and Magic: The Gathering pace complexity through rarity {{fSmaqjbU2xg#6}} {{lGm8cZC8Lfk#2}}. See [04. Learning and discovery](04-learning-and-discovery.md).

**12. Make the structure behind your randomness perceptible, or players will learn patterns that are not there.**
Emilia Lazer-Walker found that when players cannot model how inputs shape a generator, its output is indistinguishable from randomness, and cut Computational Flaneur's many sensors down to simple GPS regions {{MNEG1sLSRcM#8}}. Isaac Karth recommends foregrounding causality, as NetHack does by tracking the source of every damage instance for the death message {{MHEkE7nPuZc#17}}. The failure is well documented: Rogue's players believed some games were "scroll heavy" when no such system existed {{IaB7iQhts_c#5}}, Evan Debenham finds that uniform random ranges lead players to see patterns that are not there {{kaojiu9syPE#2}}, and Max Kreminski warns that early streaks of true randomness give players a false picture of a generator before they have learned its baseline {{66O6WDcT8KY#4}}. See [04. Learning and discovery](04-learning-and-discovery.md).

**13. Choose what to hide, make everything else deducible inside the game, and make guesses cheap to test.**
Thomas Biskup calls it lazy design to make players look mechanics up in FAQs, and admits ADOM is guilty of it {{hnoF_bL-HjI#7}}; Jim Shepard says players should never need to go online to work out how to progress {{FZn4NeQOcFE#19}}, and, of NPCs the player must woo, that guesswork is not gameplay {{LKCRim02opc#13}}. Mystery still has a place when it is chosen: Alexei Pepers separates productive uncertainty from the unproductive kind {{H5EU9_Gdu94#12}}, asks for very clear communication everywhere else {{H5EU9_Gdu94#5}}, and warns that suspense turns to frustration when testing a hypothesis is unfun or costly {{H5EU9_Gdu94#7}}. Jongwoo Kim's The Shrouded Isle makes hidden information fair with consistent rules and a way to investigate afterwards {{oV5PjvcVky8#12}}, and Nic Tringali's The Banished Vault shows every resource location while leaving its cost uncertain {{nSJA2yVsTFM#9}}. Spoilers follow anything fixed: John Harris notes randomness only delays them {{KY-TmRw5sR8#11}}, which is why Caves of Qud generates disease cures each game and records them in an in-game book {{zDTQM0Ipiz4#4}}. See [04. Learning and discovery](04-learning-and-discovery.md).

**14. Make threats readable and the odds countable, so the player is planning rather than guessing.**
Dan Norder describes the proto-roguelike Chase getting all its tactics from robots that always take the shortest route, so players can predict them exactly {{ohH6LBM2EiU#1}}; Tyriq Plummer credits Spelunky's forethought to simple, consistent enemies and traps {{OVSi3NzA0KM#6}}; Into the Breach is a fully visible puzzle {{rNi5nO5yqLs#0}}; and Scrubbin' Trubble displays enemy threat zones {{UNBAoc0TJSs#2}}. Albert Ford wants field of view symmetric and consistent with targeting {{y1zkrTcNJbc#0}}, Dylan White keeps Rift Wizard's damage formula simple enough to count hits exactly {{VVRomN99Ygw#8}}, and Evan Debenham finds randomness frustrates less when players have levers over the odds, such as Shattered Pixel Dungeon's guaranteed hits from stealth {{kaojiu9syPE#12}} {{kaojiu9syPE#11}}. Nauticrawl shows the failure: in its real-time beta players died without understanding why, until the game created turns from its simulation {{wxmio1S3iN0#5}} {{wxmio1S3iN0#7}}. See [06. Combat and tactics](06-combat-and-tactics.md) and [08. Monsters, NPCs and AI](08-monsters-npcs-and-ai.md).

**15. Watch newcomers play, and do not let veterans alone tell you what is clear.**
Andrea Roberts finds that much roguelike difficulty is knowledge veterans carry without noticing {{WtdCY6NxX6I#2}}: Roundguard's newcomers did not grasp that enemies hit back on contact {{WtdCY6NxX6I#3}}, Darren Grey describes FTL punishing players who bring a standard RPG's heroism {{C2sm-z7Ag4U#4}}, and Tabea Iseli found that a non-violent game wipes out much of players' learned game literacy {{ZJAgY73c8bA#4}}. Thomas Biskup saw ADOM's interface, praised for years by its community, condemned on Steam {{hnoF_bL-HjI#1}}, and says an hour of observed play tests with no explanation taught more than twenty years of ADOM {{hnoF_bL-HjI#12}}. Watch rather than ask, since Max Sahin finds people are bad at naming their experience {{Tll23vq6j1s#0}}, and seek out players unlike you: Alexei Pepers says only visually impaired testers show what blind players need {{e8Itfp3MHQ4#6}}, and Michael Langford simulates pre-literate children by swapping a font for unreadable glyphs {{wPN5bPxWXm8#6}}. See [04. Learning and discovery](04-learning-and-discovery.md), [13. Interface and accessibility](13-interface-and-accessibility.md) and [16. Production and process](16-production-and-process.md).

### Systems, tactics and builds

**16. Build depth from interacting systems that follow one set of rules for player, monster and world, and write those interactions between properties, not between pairs of things.**
Brian Walker describes a roguelike as a chain of exciting situations, each a cognitively interesting puzzle {{lfb6uehmrWo#1}}, and Darren Grey and Dan Recht trace those always-new problems to items and systems that interact and force trade-offs {{C2sm-z7Ag4U#3}} {{FnJxkId-1eE#0}}; Alice Lai counts such combinations as content alongside areas and monsters {{pXib0WTfLbI#2}}. Dwarf Fortress defines every creature, the player included, with the same kind of file and rules {{Zh6dnx9FM1g#12}}, Plummer calls Spelunky's shared rule set for players, NPCs and items what makes it a roguelike {{OVSi3NzA0KM#5}}, and Bob Nystrom has player input and monster AI both produce the same Action objects {{JxI3Eu5DPwE#7}}. To keep this tractable, NetHack reduces things to shared attributes such as materials and codes interactions between attributes {{QBZkvtGucBU#8}}, immersive sims have objects emit and respond to stimuli instead of hand-coding brittle pairwise cases {{wtlLa7BGZcs#1}}, and Ultimate ADOM derives abilities from body parts, so a creature without hands cannot open doors with no special code {{fGLJC5UY2o4#14}}. See [05. Systems and emergence](05-systems-and-emergence.md) and [15. Architecture and tools](15-architecture-and-tools.md).

**17. For every simulated reaction, ask how the player will see it and what decision it creates.**
Brian Walker prefers rich terrain to monsters because it explains itself and supports satisfying plans {{lfb6uehmrWo#13}}, and Darren Grey describes Brogue letting a weak character be powerful by luring monsters into lava or setting fields alight {{C2sm-z7Ag4U#15}}. Arvi Teikari reports the limit from Noita: many physically plausible reactions are interesting in themselves but hard to turn into decisions, and unlimited destruction quickly became boring {{huMHQGr8PgM#8}} {{huMHQGr8PgM#7}}. Rich Wilson expects the hard work to move onto designers as fine-grained simulation becomes feasible {{wtlLa7BGZcs#16}}, and Tyriq Plummer limited cascading effects in a multiplayer game because everyone had to stop and parse what had happened {{UNBAoc0TJSs#4}}. See [05. Systems and emergence](05-systems-and-emergence.md).

**18. Give every turn a real choice from a small set of verbs, and let movement do most of the work.**
Sersa Victory describes a turn as one mutually exclusive choice {{TeURjrAYFEU#5}}, and Darius Kazemi points to Hoplite, which offers only three or four choices but no move without consequences {{CLi4PWxDmAI#13}}. Michael Brough calls Rogue's bump-to-attack an elegant foundation, and shows its shared input letting Nightmare Cooperative move a whole party with one key {{X4be22WoVgg#0}} {{X4be22WoVgg#3}}. NetHack keeps movement as the core command and hides rarer verbs in menus {{QBZkvtGucBU#7}}, Brogue puts stairs in alcoves so that stepping onto them uses them {{Uo9-IcHhq_w#11}}, and slashie argues for triggering actions contextually by moving rather than through menus {{R8DSq8qSJ44@32392#3}}. Thomas Biskup aims to get Ultimate ADOM by with about seven keys, noting that about 90% of ADOM players die within roughly 3,000 turns and never need the power commands {{hnoF_bL-HjI#4}}. See [06. Combat and tactics](06-combat-and-tactics.md) and [13. Interface and accessibility](13-interface-and-accessibility.md).

**19. Give each monster and hazard one clear trick that targets something other than hit points, and tie it into the game's other systems.**
Brian Walker compares monsters and effects to chess pieces that project distinct zones of jeopardy {{lfb6uehmrWo#15}}; John Harris lists Shiren's monsters, each a distinct problem, from archers to one that turns your items into rice balls {{0MdNwIHwXy0#3}}; and Eric Billingsley keeps a small roster with one ability each so there is variety players can learn {{3S6uRMCdvNs#2}}. Readable behaviour can be very cheap: David Craddock notes that Rogue's monsters had minimal AI, yet players ascribed behaviour to them and learned to exploit what little there was {{Gs9Nb5ZYRY0#1}}, while Jeff Olson's lasting regret about Alphaman is monster AI with too little variation {{Gk5uWXBANkA#7}}. Traps show what goes wrong otherwise: Reed Lockwood calls the hidden, instant, single-tile damage trap a hit-point tax and makes tying traps into other systems the most important rule {{ymgmhtkRLyY#1}} {{ymgmhtkRLyY#7}}, Angband's traps degenerated once every character could detect them easily {{LaOcggk7U88#7}}, and Sersa Victory and Joris Dormans make combinations of hazards the challenge {{NjIduRLJL7U#18}}. See [06. Combat and tactics](06-combat-and-tactics.md) and [08. Monsters, NPCs and AI](08-monsters-npcs-and-ai.md).

**20. Store character power in what the player finds, so the run changes shape as it goes and avoiding a fight becomes a strategy.**
Michael Brough traces the genre's basic arc to a rogue who becomes, in effect, a wizard by collecting magic items {{zDKheM-E45I#8}} {{JpX2wWwhKdE#7}}. Brian Walker replaced Brogue's skills with items and enchantment scrolls and removed experience points, so builds based on avoidance and trickery work {{lfb6uehmrWo#5}} {{lfb6uehmrWo#7}}; Into Ruins does the same, and Eric Billingsley credits the change with more build variety and viable fleeing while conceding the loss of the feeling of growing stronger by killing {{3S6uRMCdvNs#7}}; and John Harris praises Brogue for it {{KY-TmRw5sR8#1}}. Item-based power is not the only answer: bhauth argues that Brogue's linear upgrade costs push players into one or two investments, while DCSS's skill costs support more viable builds {{tg-CjNodlmY#9}}. See [07. Characters, items and builds](07-characters-items-and-builds.md).

**21. Let chance decide what is on offer and the player decide how deep to invest, and make each offer change how the run plays.**
Andrew Cedotal finds that neither fully constructed nor fully random upgrades sustain engagement, and recommends a "drafting mullet": chance limits the options, the player controls investment, as pick-one-of-three choices do {{Gxn_MA3-Vgo#7}} {{Gxn_MA3-Vgo#8}}. Lisa Brown describes players with low strategy motivation, Brown included, who want a small number of meaningful, reactive picks at regular points, such as Slay the Spire's post-combat card choice {{BjqZdMW8U9Q#6}}. Shawn Main asks that each choice be strong enough to change the style of the run and have no answer that is right regardless of the build {{WLBJJTc6LWI#6}} {{WLBJJTc6LWI#7}}, and turning an option down should cost something, as in Brogue's treasure rooms {{TeURjrAYFEU#6}} or Oath's either-or choices whose road not taken can haunt the player {{HgnvMBVKP0M#15}}. See [07. Characters, items and builds](07-characters-items-and-builds.md).

**22. Make synergies implicit and the build space well connected, and cap how much one run can absorb.**
Cedotal finds players credit more "comboness" to synergies that arise through other game systems than to ones printed on the upgrade {{Gxn_MA3-Vgo#1}}, and Ezra Szanton orders synergy sources from obvious callouts to obscure shared choices {{JfNeYtH7xI4#8}}. Shawn Main points to Dicey Dungeons, where choices act on shared building blocks so they interact readily {{WLBJJTc6LWI#5}}. Szanton warns that isolated synergy clusters make drafting shallow, because a committed player ignores everything outside the cluster, and wants a well-connected network with weak clusters {{JfNeYtH7xI4#4}} {{JfNeYtH7xI4#5}}. At the other end, Alice Lai found that too many upgrades in one Hades run blend every build into "the same powerful sludge" {{pXib0WTfLbI#8}}. See [07. Characters, items and builds](07-characters-items-and-builds.md).

**23. Let a rare, earned build break the game, but remove any option so strong that every run must take it.**
Dan DiIorio warns against balancing a single-player roguelike like a competitive game, because players have more fun becoming more powerful than their enemies {{fSmaqjbU2xg#3}}; Courtney Liessen notes that a broken combo lasts only until the run ends {{BRJEK20iM7o#7}} and that players delight in "cheating" the system as designed {{BRJEK20iM7o#0}}; and the Against the Storm team deliberately sought the rare game-breaking build {{tWFIQaeSs6M#2}}. Brian Walker keeps effects strong and balances them by situation rather than by shrinking numbers {{lfb6uehmrWo#8}}. The line falls at dominance: Pandamander warns against strategies that win every time {{KtQh4QrsUzs#7}}, Against the Storm cut city walls because they would have been built every run {{tWFIQaeSs6M#5}}, and Andrew Aversa links Tangledeep's contradictory negative reviews to jobs far stronger or weaker than the rest {{Gluz5nIplcA#14}}. See [03. Death, difficulty and progression](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md) and [07. Characters, items and builds](07-characters-items-and-builds.md).

### Generating worlds and content

**24. Decide what each generator should do to the player before you pick a technique, and budget for it as design work, not free content.**
Isaac Karth asks designers to say why they want to generate something and what effect it should have {{MHEkE7nPuZc#0}}, Gillian Smith to choose generative systems like materials, by the experience you want {{R8DSq8qSJ44@3555#7}}, and Alexei Pepers not to use procedural generation for every problem just because it exists {{mYMdMAvTHpo#0}}. The talks agree it does not save work: Brian Walker calls procedural level design a different skill, not an easier one {{Uo9-IcHhq_w#1}}; Bruno Dias says generation is not a force multiplier for cheap content {{UGPkEWqwSAY#7}}; Younès Rabii measures a generator's real cost in the designer knowledge encoded in it {{BJ_84d7v3K4#3}}; and the Wildermyth team warn that honing generated content to quality takes substantial time {{vtU3Xa0ShKw#21}}. What goes wrong otherwise is concrete: Santiago Zapata's detective game failed for lack of authored content to feed its engine {{TMxzVy7-VYQ#11}}, and Gabriel Koenig found Jettomero's procedural systems added almost nothing because they ran entirely behind the scenes {{tmrYfnMrifw#12}}. See [10. Content generation](10-content-generation.md) and [11. Generative methods](11-generative-methods.md).

**25. Make variation players can perceive: commit each level or region to one strong idea, correlate what you generate, and keep a plain baseline so the rare stands out.**
Many talks independently warn that a generator can produce endless outputs players cannot tell apart, Kate Compton's "10,000 bowls of oatmeal" {{MHEkE7nPuZc#6}} {{TvlZinAvpwg#8}} {{CLi4PWxDmAI#0}} {{HgnvMBVKP0M#11}}. Rich Wilson found that mixing many small random elements into every level made them indistinguishable, so Prey: Mooncrash gave each level one dominant hazard {{wtlLa7BGZcs#9}}; Isaac Karth points to No Man's Sky's single-biome planets and to landmark levels {{MHEkE7nPuZc#4}} {{MHEkE7nPuZc#12}}. Alex Byaly shows that generating attributes independently makes every region a sample of the same distribution, and points to Tales of Maj'Eyal's correlated item bundles {{otEv5dh2f4E#0}} {{otEv5dh2f4E#3}}. And outliers need ordinary neighbours: Cat Manning wants a few spiky characters against an unremarkable cast {{q7E1N-fJnrA#8}}, Elliot Trinidad gives Blaseball plenty of plain names so the wild ones stand out {{9yFferxbrBU#4}}, and Mini and Max's rare "atoms" extend novelty long after the common ones are familiar {{NlxGPKV0PrY#8}}. See [09. Level generation](09-level-generation.md), [10. Content generation](10-content-generation.md) and [11. Generative methods](11-generative-methods.md).

**26. Author the structure and generate the detail: give the generator a plan, and hand-made pieces wherever judgement matters.**
Dead Cells abandoned generated platforms because gravity makes jumps hard to get right, and hand-authors its rooms at two to five minutes each {{QbTnKvRrNYY#3}} {{QbTnKvRrNYY#5}}, assembling them on a simple tree of nodes for entrance, exits, shop and treasure {{QbTnKvRrNYY#8}}. Spelunky's rooms are mostly fixed tiles {{CLi4PWxDmAI#6}}, Sergio Garces picks from about a hundred quickly authored layouts {{6U8c6hbVJLY#7}}, and NetHack mixes generated levels with fixed branches and special levels {{rIB0y_kwFuY#3}}. Joris Dormans gives the generator a plan as a graph of locks, keys and relationships {{NjIduRLJL7U#9}} {{b2VkgcC1xRc#7}}, but warns that levels planned entirely in advance become fragile machines that collapse when the player deviates {{NjIduRLJL7U#12}}. Local methods need the same frame: wave function collapse output looks the same in every direction, so Caves of Qud wraps its ruins in a higher-level structure {{owmGknfj6H4#18}}, and Emily Halina shows such methods producing dungeons with missing doors or unreachable rooms {{KWBpQz1mHcA#2}}. See [09. Level generation](09-level-generation.md) and [11. Generative methods](11-generative-methods.md).

**27. A generated space matters only if it creates decisions: size it from your movement rules, and give it loops.**
John Harris argues that if a random dungeon's contents do not challenge the player, its layout is meaningless {{KY-TmRw5sR8#5}}. Brian Walker builds space from overlapping zones with different advantages {{lfb6uehmrWo#10}}, Loren Schmidt likens reading terrain to reading a Go board {{FaLUhh_Vfmk#0}}, and Sersa Victory tunes generators to layer several choices into one encounter {{TeURjrAYFEU#13}}. Space must fit the rules that move through it: Chogue widened Rogue's corridors so bishops could leave the first room {{l1YEJBKehAY#3}}, Wildermyth's generated maps failed large monsters that could not fit through narrow paths {{vtU3Xa0ShKw#15}}, and Scrubbin' Trubble uses small stages so cooperating players stay together {{UNBAoc0TJSs#3}}. Trees and perfect mazes force one route; Brogue adds doors where two sides of a wall are far apart by path distance {{Uo9-IcHhq_w#3}}, and Larn's imperfect mazes give navigation choices {{b45orGdFwi0#14}}. See [09. Level generation](09-level-generation.md).

**28. Guarantee whatever blocks progress, then look at your generator's output in bulk and in context.**
Herbert Wolverson's rule is to guide your randomness and then check the result {{TlLIOgWYVpI#18}}, culling unreachable regions with a Dijkstra map {{TlLIOgWYVpI#14}}; Jack Schlesinger uses a flood fill against dead ends {{5PF9RB0_ifM#5}}, and Brian Walker tolerates an occasional inaccessible reward room but never an unreachable staircase {{Uo9-IcHhq_w#17}}. Randomly placed key items can create circular dependencies that make a game unbeatable {{C7laugXEc9s#4}}, so Jon Perry gives almost every gate in Planet Zoldath more than one solution {{NlxGPKV0PrY#16}}. Beyond correctness, expressive range analysis (scoring many outputs on metrics chosen for your game and plotting them) shows what a generator actually makes {{_s-Tn0biyEk#0}} {{_s-Tn0biyEk#2}}, and Oliver Withington uses the plot to choose outliers and cluster representatives to inspect by hand {{Ls9wksddN5c#6}}. Mike Cook adds that a generator is best understood by how its output collides with the game's other systems {{nOGNdrjm8ek#1}}, and Schlesinger and droqen use solvers to judge the difficulty of generated puzzles {{5PF9RB0_ifM#14}} {{KfYWVLV5UxQ#7}}. See [09. Level generation](09-level-generation.md) and [11. Generative methods](11-generative-methods.md).

**29. Keep a human between the generator and the player, and plan for the meanings players will find in what it makes.**
Ben Berman observes that influential generated content usually relied on human curation {{Z6lHExfem6U#18}}, Martin Pichlmair finds delight in generators where a person performs an activity and selects the result {{EMb6B2cIJps#4}}, and Gabriel Koenig's Test Tube Titans produced terrible creatures when the same process ran without the player choosing {{xju91VGh2Ps#10}}. Robin Sloan rules out piping a language model's raw output to players, and heavily filters the quests generated for Sloan's own project {{cIpErjWBqm0#6}}, and Janelle Shane would not expose a generator to open player input, preferring to generate and hand-pick in advance {{VhUh9rksfaE#6}}, and Everest Pipkin calls choosing the source text the most important decision in generative text {{IYNKs8vfocc#4}}. Players will read meaning into the output whether or not you meant any: Blaseball's fans formed deep attachments to players that were only names and a few facts {{9yFferxbrBU#0}} {{zO05p-e57AQ#18}}, Younès Rabii's flower generator produced shapes resembling fascist imagery {{s6Kxh_Zyiqs#6}}, and Darius Kazemi recommends layered filters and a public takedown policy {{CLi4PWxDmAI#14}}. See [10. Content generation](10-content-generation.md) and [11. Generative methods](11-generative-methods.md).

### Story, tone and meaning

**30. Build for the stories your systems produce, and leave gaps for the player to fill.**
Emilia Lazer-Walker argues roguelikes are better than any other genre at emergent storytelling {{MNEG1sLSRcM#0}}, and Tarn Adams notes that players retell what they did with buckets in Skyrim, never the scripted dragon fights {{4-7TtPX5uhg#5}}. Adams names the conditions: mechanics that interlock, stay internally consistent and can be investigated {{4-7TtPX5uhg#1}}; Jim Shepard adds that Dungeonmans' tone gives players a trellis for their own stories {{_Si626-Y_q0#0}}. Rogue's creators planned rich monster lives and found that players imagined the rest from rooms, letters and stats {{IaB7iQhts_c#6}}, Dustin Freeman presents bare events so as not to take the player's interpretation away {{RYZSdPuvta8#2}}, and Paul Dean argues that players can get the gist of a story from fragments, as in archaeology or a jigsaw {{nLqLOzrjkYU#12}}. Abstraction helps but does not work alone: Evan Ovadia found ASCII needs a small trigger of detail to evoke imagination {{Xwa1KbXqeGQ#3}}. See [12. Narrative and worldbuilding](12-narrative-and-worldbuilding.md) and [14. Art and audio](14-art-and-audio.md).

**31. Choose what to simulate knowing that players read your numbers and systems as facts about the world, and make fiction and mechanics push the same way.**
Xalavier Nelson Jr. argues that a game's generation systems and base numbers say more about its world than its writing does, and that even deciding what counted as an organ would be read as objective truth {{SdQxLThAbs8#7}} {{SdQxLThAbs8#1}}. Ivy Melinda relays how Caves of Qud's inherited roguelike systems created a "combat gravity well" that made the game more bloodthirsty than intended {{db6S2lgTWmI#3}}; Emilia Lazer-Walker warns that if your main verb is kill, players will resolve emergent situations with violence {{MNEG1sLSRcM#12}}; and Jongwoo Kim found that standard simulation models assume material growth, which undermined a story about belief {{oV5PjvcVky8#1}}. Tim Keenan attributes the dislike of curated story in roguelikes to story and mechanics pulling in different directions {{G5rq5ei2Lec#1}}; done well, fiction sells a rule, as when Against the Storm turned a bare timer into an impatient queen {{tWFIQaeSs6M#14}}. See [05. Systems and emergence](05-systems-and-emergence.md) and [12. Narrative and worldbuilding](12-narrative-and-worldbuilding.md).

**32. Write authored story for repetition and death: keep what replays short, release story across runs, and acknowledge what happened to each character.**
Tim Keenan and Andrew Aversa both warn that story replayed every run becomes a burden {{G5rq5ei2Lec#2}} {{kn2QkH5lByw#5}}. Nic Junius finds branching narratives become untenable to author, and uses braided linear storylines released by conditions, with a cap on new story per run like Hades' {{sOZH6RPuNJ8#0}} {{sOZH6RPuNJ8#2}} {{sOZH6RPuNJ8#5}}; Voyageur and Sunshine Shuffle use storylets gated by conditions {{UGPkEWqwSAY#11}} {{MyVigc1nDig#1}}. Death itself is material: Harry Solomons calls the restart a powerful narrative tool {{AwOuZTCeOrU#10}}, and Duskers keeps discovered logs across deaths {{G5rq5ei2Lec#3}}. Wildermyth makes generated heroes feel authored by having generation lead into handwritten content and by acknowledging what happened to them by chance {{vtU3Xa0ShKw#0}} {{vtU3Xa0ShKw#14}}. See [12. Narrative and worldbuilding](12-narrative-and-worldbuilding.md).

**33. Log what your simulation does, sift it for drama, and give the player instruments to find it.**
Max Kreminski presents James Ryan's model in which emergent narrative is what results after events are chronicled, sifted and narrativised, not the raw output {{BDcZSWHu8RQ#7}}, and Brianna McHorse and Chris Foster agree that fluent, connected events do not by themselves make a story {{aJC3ioashbs#1}}. Allie Signet and Joe Maliksi of SIBR say surprising stories stay impenetrable without detailed records and that filtering is what turns data into narrative {{WeRCGy3-uPY#0}} {{WeRCGy3-uPY#1}}; Kreminski's story sifters search a chronicle for sequences to surface {{oaDI0lWkedg#2}}. Without instruments, players are lost in a deluge of insignificant events {{BDcZSWHu8RQ#6}}, so Jasmine Otto lets players aim their attention {{NjIDVIIg1lY#0}}, Jonathan Lessard publishes a live, hyperlinked chronicle {{D8A9dpITLAU#2}}, and Crusader Kings III, as Cat Manning describes, sums up hidden personality as a two-word epithet {{q7E1N-fJnrA#9}}. See [12. Narrative and worldbuilding](12-narrative-and-worldbuilding.md) and [13. Interface and accessibility](13-interface-and-accessibility.md).

### Interface, craft and process

**34. Put the depth in the rules, not in operating them: plan the interface from the start, never make the player wait, and automate decisions that have one right answer.**
Subset Games estimate that half or more of their development time goes to UX, and found early Into the Breach playtests painful because of presentation, not design {{rNi5nO5yqLs#2}}; Angband's maintainers made it approachable without making it easier {{eeSk665OXkM#6}}. Thomas Biskup keeps depth in the game logic and out of the interface {{hnoF_bL-HjI#6}}, designing Ultimate ADOM's interface simple-first because reducing ADOM's complex one proved a constant struggle {{iZUXjbcAfYs#25}}, and Josh Ge makes every action in Cogmind reachable by mouse {{9se2M27LuOs#10}}. Jeremiah Reid's rule is that animation never blocks input {{xSYVQc7cH-4#6}}, and Josh Ge, Angband and Dungeon Crawl Stone Soup automate no-brainer decisions, one-key targeting and exploration {{9se2M27LuOs#13}} {{eeSk665OXkM#8}} {{tg-CjNodlmY#2}}. See [13. Interface and accessibility](13-interface-and-accessibility.md).

**35. Treat representation as a budget and an audience choice, and make whatever you draw read at a glance and hold together as one style.**
Santiago Zapata, Evan Ovadia and Josh Ge all note that characters make content cheap: a new monster costs a glyph, a colour, a description and its data {{ZMdxP7fO6wE#7}} {{Xwa1KbXqeGQ#6}} {{jviNpRGuCIU#14}}. Graphics reach a larger audience but cost accordingly: Zapata and Thomas Biskup say mainstream players expect them {{TMxzVy7-VYQ#12}} {{hnoF_bL-HjI#8}}, Zapata calls static sprites in place of letters a half-measure {{ZMdxP7fO6wE#8}}, and adding graphics to ADOM took three and a half to four of the revival's five years {{5CQP8ndTjEY#28}}. Whatever you draw should read like a symbol: Joel Ryan gives tiles a few strong forms {{UIsNv3UOzvA#14}}, and Subset and Marlowe Dobbe derive a creature's look from what it does {{rNi5nO5yqLs#12}} {{Wi-OHFmW4Ew#3}}. And keep one style: Sergio Garces and Ovadia both warn that mixing assets from sources with different styles produces art that clashes {{6U8c6hbVJLY#2}} {{Xwa1KbXqeGQ#2}}. See [14. Art and audio](14-art-and-audio.md).

**36. Write the rules once and author content as data, so that adding one more item takes minutes rather than days.**
Kate Compton's rule is to author many pieces as static data run through one well-tested interpreter {{isB82kORimk#5}}, and the talks show the same shape in Ultimate ADOM, Caves of Qud, Cataclysm, Hades and Backpack Hero {{iZUXjbcAfYs#13}} {{n6mKigS_71Y#0}} {{ziN33iMQVPo#4}} {{pXib0WTfLbI#10}} {{COeJTtSDd78#2}}; Spry Fox's tags let a newly tagged item flow into every loot table and shop automatically {{rWPJ5fW1UH8#2}}. Know the limits of your format: Monster Train's one-script-per-effect cards broke down on dependent, scaling effects, leaving behaviour implicit in code and forcing custom code for specific cards {{qO3CIpP62Q0#7}}. The payoff is speed: Thomas Robertson notes that if adding fire is easy you will add ice too {{f1t4yPjfZyM#6}}, Danny Day built two new Desktop Dungeons classes in a week {{p48ArjJweSo#14}}, and Thomas Biskup added a spell to Ultimate ADOM in about eight minutes once the feature beneath it existed {{fGLJC5UY2o4#3}}. Data files also let players mod the game {{mES03vaZo3U#7}} {{9se2M27LuOs#1}}. See [15. Architecture and tools](15-architecture-and-tools.md) and [17. Community and business](17-community-and-business.md).

**37. Seal a deterministic, serialisable simulation off from its presentation, and do it before you need it.**
Brian Cronin separates data, state, logic and presentation, keeps all state including the random number generator in one serialisable structure, and lets presentation learn about the world only from emitted change records {{jhcHlg7YhPg#3}} {{jhcHlg7YhPg#6}} {{jhcHlg7YhPg#8}}; saves, replays and networking then follow {{jhcHlg7YhPg#10}}, and a preview is just the real code run on a copy {{jhcHlg7YhPg#11}}. Generation belongs inside the same discipline: Andrew Clifton derives random values from a hash of the situation and the game seed {{e4b--cyXEsM#0}} {{e4b--cyXEsM#1}}, and Alexander Birke keeps Sea of Rifts deterministic so players' bugs can be reproduced {{0jFzBf0mCRY#1}}. The cost of waiting is reported repeatedly: Monster Train's lack of determinism forced many preview exceptions {{qO3CIpP62Q0#5}}, Danny Day found replays too complicated to retrofit {{p48ArjJweSo#13}}, and ADOM's interface runs through all its code, which made a mobile version practically impossible {{iZUXjbcAfYs#6}}. See [15. Architecture and tools](15-architecture-and-tools.md) and [11. Generative methods](11-generative-methods.md).

**38. Scope to finish: ship something small first, prototype the core cheaply, cut whatever does not serve it, and multiply your estimate by four, then by four again.**
Josh Ge, Santiago Zapata, Jeff Lait and slashie all argue for finishing small games, because finishing is how you learn the whole process and get feedback {{jviNpRGuCIU#2}} {{TMxzVy7-VYQ#1}} {{LQPGmxytTws#1}} {{R8DSq8qSJ44@32392#1}}. Ge starts from a core mechanic explainable in one sentence {{jviNpRGuCIU#4}}, Subset from a feeling {{rNi5nO5yqLs#7}}, and Tyler Robertson and Brian Cronin prototype in spreadsheets and Tabletop Simulator first {{hdhKErNIYgE#0}} {{Kh9m-ixeqec#0}}. Matt Ritchie and Eric Billingsley cut anything that does not add to the core, including genre staples {{KKwgKTbiEY0#3}} {{3S6uRMCdvNs#4}}, and Against the Storm declined features, even fantastic ones, that did not make runs differ {{tWFIQaeSs6M#4}}. Tyler Coleman's rule is to multiply any estimate by four and then by four again {{C6jR41fZjqM#2}}; turning Cogmind from a 7DRL, a game made for the Seven Day Roguelike competition {{NaqP13nkpfU#0}}, into a commercial one was expected to take a year and took nearly 6,000 hours over three and a half years {{9se2M27LuOs#6}}. See [16. Production and process](16-production-and-process.md).

**39. Treat what players tell you as a description of a problem, not a specification of the fix, and make sure someone triages all of it.**
Brian Cronin credits consistent triage, someone reading every report, as the main tool behind Monster Train's success {{qO3CIpP62Q0#10}}. Evan Debenham works out the desire behind a suggestion {{NMWQIdfCgQg#9}}, Andrew Aversa listens to the problem players describe but not necessarily the solution {{Gluz5nIplcA#12}}, Subset diagnoses whether a complaint is about design, presentation or a wish for a different game {{rNi5nO5yqLs#4}}, and Chris King insists feedback is not a democracy {{1Np1DSdpoR0#7}}. Before changing course, weigh the quiet players: Angband's maintainers and Dan DiIorio both warn that the satisfied majority is silent {{eeSk665OXkM#18}} {{fSmaqjbU2xg#1}}. And tend the community on purpose, since, as Caelyn Sandel warns, one left to form by itself is run by its loudest, meanest voices {{NANqgqeqh2k#9}}. See [16. Production and process](16-production-and-process.md) and [17. Community and business](17-community-and-business.md).

**40. Build the means of sharing a run into the game: logs, morgue files, seeds and data players can pass around.**
Tariq, the developer of Catacomb Kids, argues that procedural generation loses much of its point without a community to compare runs with {{SDojcmObQzw#0}}, and Ivy Melinda finds the gaps between players' different experiences of Caves of Qud fertile ground for fan work {{db6S2lgTWmI#5}}. Signet and Maliksi ask for scrollback and replays that are easy to view and share, and for safe public data {{WeRCGy3-uPY#7}} {{WeRCGy3-uPY#9}}; Alan Malloy describes DCSS saving a morgue file for every online game, which makes help requests answerable {{QV57CYrIZow#3}}; and Mike Cook asks designers to inspect output at the cropped scale players actually share {{nOGNdrjm8ek#10}}. Drew Streib reports that live watching on public servers shortened NetHack's learning curve from years to weeks for many players {{J4RK-3zW8vY#3}}. See [17. Community and business](17-community-and-business.md).

## Fault lines

These are the questions where credible practitioners in the talks chose differently, and where the right answer depends on your game. Each is developed in the chapter's "Tensions and open questions" section.

**Should death be permanent by default?** Andrew Aversa argues permadeath should be the default only if you can deliver its promise of a varied, fun early game without a heavy opening story {{kn2QkH5lByw#11}}. Against that, David Brevik resisted Blizzard's request to make Diablo real-time and drop permadeath, because turn-based play and permadeath were where a roguelike's tension came from {{OwFrTY3IoQQ#2}}, Todd Furmanski values the precarious power narrative it creates {{l9eE4mT8xqI#4}}, and Glenn Wichman points out that irreversibility is what makes decisions real {{IaB7iQhts_c#15}}. It depends on where your best content is: at the start of every run, or at the end of a long one. See [03. Death, difficulty and progression](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md).

**Should runs make the character stronger?** Shawn Main argues players want their time rewarded {{WLBJJTc6LWI#15}}, and Jim Shepard that meta power softens permadeath and frees players to experiment {{FZn4NeQOcFE#5}}. Arvi Teikari's Noita relies on growing knowledge rather than unlocking more powerful items {{huMHQGr8PgM#15}}; Tim Keenan notes that purists find meta-progression grindy, though others like runs that differ because of what they have earned {{R8DSq8qSJ44@30756#5}}; Aversa that it frustrates when it feels like required dying {{kn2QkH5lByw#12}}; and John Harris considers the Wii Shiren, which kept levels after death, the weakest in its series {{0MdNwIHwXy0#11}}. It depends on whether your systems produce enough novelty for knowledge alone to keep runs fresh. See [03. Death, difficulty and progression](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md).

**How much should randomness be smoothed?** Shawn Main warns that smoothing out variance makes a game flat {{UZ7XqQYDM7o#14}}, and Dylan Gedig accepts reduced agency in Peglin for memorable moments {{ke0cLTVg2_M#10}}; Evan Debenham and Sébastien Benard smooth deliberately {{kaojiu9syPE#1}} {{QbTnKvRrNYY#13}}. The tie-breaker offered at John Harris's talk is whether players can build intuition for the randomness {{KY-TmRw5sR8#14}}. Two cautions cut across both camps: Ben Berman warns pity systems can be addictive for people with gambling problems {{Z6lHExfem6U#17}}, and Tyler Coleman that hidden fixed cycles may verge on dark patterns {{C6jR41fZjqM#13}}. See [03. Death, difficulty and progression](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md).

**How hard should the clock push?** Tyriq Plummer removed starvation from Catacomb Kids because it punished caution and made pacifist runs impossible {{OVSi3NzA0KM#11}}, and Thomas Biskup made Ultimate ADOM's hunger weaken but never kill {{hnoF_bL-HjI#11}}. Andrew Aversa's Tangledeep collapsed into kiting without a clock {{Gluz5nIplcA#7}}, and Nicholas Feinberg keeps Crawl's clocks weak because strong ones would push optimal players to turn off auto-explore {{b8uoQHedFV0#15}}. It depends on whether something else in your game already makes waiting costly. See [03. Death, difficulty and progression](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md) and [06. Combat and tactics](06-combat-and-tactics.md).

**How much should stay hidden, and what about the wiki?** Alexei Pepers, Arvi Teikari and Leonard Richardson make the case for deliberate mystery, rare interactions found over many runs, and even misdirection {{H5EU9_Gdu94#12}} {{huMHQGr8PgM#12}} {{ntVjLNl2_ro#7}}; Thomas Biskup, Jim Shepard and Andrew Aversa want mechanics deducible or presented in the game {{hnoF_bL-HjI#7}} {{FZn4NeQOcFE#19}} {{Gluz5nIplcA#1}}. On spoilers, Brian Walker warns that lookups make play rote {{lfb6uehmrWo#2}} {{lfb6uehmrWo#3}}, while Jeremy Rose, Drew Streib and Max Kreminski defend outside knowledge as an encyclopedia, as insufficient to win, or as not worth fighting {{ziN33iMQVPo#8}} {{J4RK-3zW8vY#11}} {{66O6WDcT8KY#10}}. It depends on whether the mystery is the game's subject with everything else clear {{H5EU9_Gdu94#5}}, and on who is playing: low-discovery players want rules exposed up front {{BjqZdMW8U9Q#11}}. See [04. Learning and discovery](04-learning-and-discovery.md).

**Few pieces or many?** Eric Billingsley argues fewer elements force you to tune every interaction and make a more learnable game {{3S6uRMCdvNs#19}}, Michael Brough that type-specific effects matter only when there are few types {{X4be22WoVgg#7}}, and Cole Wehrle that too much content makes a game feel arbitrary {{HgnvMBVKP0M#13}}. George Moromisato traces NetHack's difficulty and endurance to its breadth {{QBZkvtGucBU#4}} {{QBZkvtGucBU#10}}, and Casey Yano's team found unlimited relics playtested better than a three-slot cap {{KYlq0Msu6hc#3}}. It depends on whether you want players to learn a system whole or spend years mastering a space. See [05. Systems and emergence](05-systems-and-emergence.md) and [10. Content generation](10-content-generation.md).

**How deep should the simulation go?** Brian Walker values terrain that explains itself and supports plans {{lfb6uehmrWo#13}}, and Todd Furmanski would spend the gap between turns simulating weather and material chemistry {{tmrYfnMrifw#7}}. Arvi Teikari found that only a small set of Noita's material reactions were immediately interesting as gameplay {{huMHQGr8PgM#8}}, Rich Wilson expects finer simulation to move the hard work onto designers {{wtlLa7BGZcs#16}}, and Cat Manning warns that characters whose moods change too fast become chaotic and frustrating {{q7E1N-fJnrA#13}}. It depends on how much of the machinery the player can observe and act on. See [05. Systems and emergence](05-systems-and-emergence.md) and [08. Monsters, NPCs and AI](08-monsters-npcs-and-ai.md).

**Keep the leak or plug it?** Jason Grinblat argues that rough edges show a possibility space too rich to file down, and Caves of Qud turned a crash-prone body-swap exploit into a feature {{kvCky6BbTuE#10}} {{kvCky6BbTuE#11}}; Blaseball adds new systems on top of broken ones {{zO05p-e57AQ#7}}. Dungeon Crawl Stone Soup excludes powerful but tedious mechanics on principle {{QV57CYrIZow#12}}, and Liza Knipscher allows no clever workarounds in a simulation's core energy economy {{jBhcHdV5HHo#13}}. It depends on whether the leak opens new experiences or only adds tedium, and whether it sits in the core that everything else rests on. See [05. Systems and emergence](05-systems-and-emergence.md).

**Hand-made or generated, and should the seam show?** Ben Berman wants generated levels that look made by hand {{Z6lHExfem6U#5}}, while Jon Perry stopped forcing Mini and Max's handcrafted areas to match its procedural ones because it made them worse {{NlxGPKV0PrY#5}}. Wildermyth weighs generated maps that multiplied cheaply against handcrafted maps that allowed pacing and unique goals but took long to make {{vtU3Xa0ShKw#15}} {{vtU3Xa0ShKw#16}}, and Sergio Garces finds hand-placing wins unless you invest heavily in the algorithm {{6U8c6hbVJLY#6}}. It depends on where authored quality matters most, and whether the generated parts sit where looking generated is acceptable. See [09. Level generation](09-level-generation.md).

**Must every generated output be good?** Herbert Wolverson checks every result {{TlLIOgWYVpI#18}}, Sébastien Benard holds that a shipped generator must produce good output essentially every time {{QbTnKvRrNYY#4}}, and Brian Walker never lets a generator block progress {{Uo9-IcHhq_w#17}}. Alexei Pepers reports that Caves of Qud keeps generators unconstrained until a specific harm is proven {{H5EU9_Gdu94#16}}, and Bjarke Alexander Larsen accepts unchecked randomness when players have a way to deal with bad outcomes {{gxXZRofE5dI#10}}. It depends on who pays for a bad output: a player who cannot finish, or one who can route around it. See [11. Generative methods](11-generative-methods.md).

**Automate the effort, or keep it?** Josh Ge, Dungeon Crawl Stone Soup and Angband automate repetitive actions with one right answer {{9se2M27LuOs#13}} {{tg-CjNodlmY#2}} {{eeSk665OXkM#8}}. Zeno Rogue leaves auto-travel out of HyperRogue because navigation is the challenge {{wOlSws-OI2o#4}}, Martin Pichlmair values physical effort as part of the experience {{EMb6B2cIJps#12}}, and Yong Zhen Zhou weighs the accessibility gained by simplifying physical inputs against the energy and fantasy it can strip away {{KRZHT-gktD0#7}}, and Vagante makes menu time a risk {{OVSi3NzA0KM#13}}. For blind players, auto-explore is what makes exploration accessible, yet it leaves them disoriented, so Alexei Pepers would offer it as an option with orientation aids {{e8Itfp3MHQ4#3}} {{e8Itfp3MHQ4#16}}. It depends on whether the effort is the game you are making, and for whom. See [13. Interface and accessibility](13-interface-and-accessibility.md).

**A shaped story, or an open one?** Tarn Adams found Dwarf Fortress's adventure mode flat without overarching structure and looks to villains' schemes for plot {{4-7TtPX5uhg#0}} {{4-7TtPX5uhg#4}}, and Matt Carney gives every run a "pulse" such as descending a dungeon {{SU-VcmsOvZo#9}}. Emilia Lazer-Walker gave up on a shaped arc as too hard {{MNEG1sLSRcM#7}}, Cole Wehrle's Oath has no narrative framing {{HgnvMBVKP0M#18}}, and Tim Keenan left Duskers without a final resolution, though its players wanted one {{G5rq5ei2Lec#7}}. Stephen G. Ware adds that emergent agent stories can only hope to hit required beats, while planners can guarantee them {{Rs_-yMsJDq8#1}}. It depends on whether your game has beats that must land, and how much you will intervene behind the player's back. See [12. Narrative and worldbuilding](12-narrative-and-worldbuilding.md).

## A design brief for a new roguelike

Answer these in order when starting a new game. Each answer should fit in a sentence or two; if one does not, that is the part of the design to prototype first.

**The promise**

1. What should a run feel like, in one sentence, and is the game mainly about mastering a challenge, inhabiting a world, or a deliberate mix? See [01](01-what-is-a-roguelike.md).
2. Who should find this game, and what will you tell them plainly: turn-based or real time, what death costs, what carries over? See [01](01-what-is-a-roguelike.md) and [17](17-community-and-business.md).
3. Which parts of the roguelike package (permadeath, generation, consumables, turns) are you keeping, and what does the job of each part you drop? See [01](01-what-is-a-roguelike.md) and [02](02-history-and-lineage.md).

**The run**

4. How long is a run, and how many seconds pass between death and the next real decision? See [03](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md).
5. Where does the character's power live, and how does the game change between the first floor and the last? See [07](07-characters-items-and-builds.md).
6. What makes waiting costly, and what would a player with infinite patience do? See [03](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md) and [06](06-combat-and-tactics.md).

**Death and what persists**

7. Which decisions matter only because they cannot be undone? See [03](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md).
8. What survives a death (knowledge, options, story, world state, power), and which loop does each reward live in? See [03](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md) and [12](12-narrative-and-worldbuilding.md).
9. What is the default mode, and which difficulty options will you offer beside it? See [03](03-death-difficulty-and-progression.md).

**What varies and what is authored**

10. Which parts are generated, which are assembled from hand-made pieces, and which are fixed? See [09](09-level-generation.md) and [10](10-content-generation.md).
11. For each generator, what should it do to the player, and where in play will they perceive the difference between two outputs? See [10](10-content-generation.md) and [11](11-generative-methods.md).

**Verbs and systems**

12. What are the verbs, can movement carry most of them, and does every turn offer a real choice? See [06](06-combat-and-tactics.md) and [13](13-interface-and-accessibility.md).
13. Which properties do your systems share, and what does each system reward and imply about the world? See [05](05-systems-and-emergence.md) and [12](12-narrative-and-worldbuilding.md).
14. How are upgrades offered, how many picks does a run contain, and is any option so strong that every run takes it? See [07](07-characters-items-and-builds.md).
15. What is each monster's one trick, and what does each trap or hazard connect to? See [06](06-combat-and-tactics.md) and [08](08-monsters-npcs-and-ai.md).

**How the player learns**

16. What separates an expert from a novice, and how does each death explain itself? See [04](04-learning-and-discovery.md).
17. What is deliberately hidden, is everything else deducible in the game, and how cheaply can a player test a guess? See [04](04-learning-and-discovery.md).

**The generator**

18. What must every output guarantee, which algorithm checks it, and which failures are you willing to tolerate? See [09](09-level-generation.md) and [11](11-generative-methods.md).
19. How will you look at a large batch of outputs, and who selects what reaches the player? See [11](11-generative-methods.md).

**Presentation and story**

20. Letters, tiles or full art: what can you afford for every monster and item, and what does your audience expect? See [14](14-art-and-audio.md).
21. Can a newcomer reach every action without memorised keys, see outcomes before committing, and act without waiting on animation? See [13](13-interface-and-accessibility.md).
22. Where will players' stories come from, and how does a finished run become something they can keep and share? See [12](12-narrative-and-worldbuilding.md) and [17](17-community-and-business.md).

**Scope and process**

23. What is the smallest version you could finish, and what is the cheapest prototype of its core? See [16](16-production-and-process.md).
24. Is the simulation deterministic, serialisable and separate from presentation, and is content data? See [15](15-architecture-and-tools.md).
25. Who will play it while you watch, and how soon? See [16](16-production-and-process.md).
