# 05. Systems and emergence

Most of a roguelike's depth comes from rules meeting each other: fire and dry grass, a wand and a monster type, a creature's body and a door. This chapter is about building rules that combine in that way. It covers why the genre depends on them, which systems to simulate and what they end up saying, how many pieces to have, and the architectures that let everything interact. It then turns to the physical, magical, economic and social systems the talks hold up as models. It ends with what to do when the result surprises you with an exploit, a runaway loop or a story nobody noticed.

The talks come at the subject from both ends. Designers of long-running games report what their systems produced and what they had to cut. Programmers and researchers describe the data structures, cellular automata and rule engines underneath. A few speakers from board games, larp and reality television show what systems look like outside the single-player dungeon. The talks agree most strongly that interactions under one consistent set of rules are what drives the genre. They disagree most sharply about how many pieces to have, how much to simulate and which surprises to keep.

## In brief

- A roguelike needs a steady supply of problems the player has not solved before, and that supply comes from interacting systems that force trade-offs, not from scripts {{lfb6uehmrWo#1}} {{C2sm-z7Ag4U#3}} {{FnJxkId-1eE#0}}.
- Count combinations as content alongside monsters and levels, because the player's space of actions always outgrows the rules you write {{pXib0WTfLbI#2}} {{3daNITP9vBc#1}} {{mES03vaZo3U#0}}.
- Make the player, monsters and objects follow one set of rules, so that a trap, an explosion or a teleporter treats them all alike and players can predict and plan {{Zh6dnx9FM1g#12}} {{OVSi3NzA0KM#5}} {{6Whz8bvjAKo#2}} {{RYZSdPuvta8#3}} {{wtlLa7BGZcs#2}}.
- To avoid an explosion of pairwise cases, code interactions between properties (materials, stimuli, body-part capabilities), not between pairs of objects {{QBZkvtGucBU#8}} {{wtlLa7BGZcs#1}} {{mES03vaZo3U#3}} {{fGLJC5UY2o4#14}} {{jBhcHdV5HHo#5}}.
- Every system says something about the world, whether or not you meant it to, so choose what to simulate and emphasise by what it rewards and what it implies {{_vf3HO3wn20#7}} {{oV5PjvcVky8#1}} {{db6S2lgTWmI#3}} {{MNEG1sLSRcM#12}}.
- Terrain and materials that explain themselves let the player plan clever uses of outside power, but ask of every simulated reaction whether the player can see it and turn it into a decision {{lfb6uehmrWo#13}} {{C2sm-z7Ag4U#15}} {{huMHQGr8PgM#8}}.
- Tie traps and hazards into the game's other systems; an element with no other connections can be cancelled by a single counter, as easy detection did to Angband's traps {{ymgmhtkRLyY#7}} {{LaOcggk7U88#7}} {{NjIduRLJL7U#16}}.
- For each resource, know how it renews, what else draws on it and what behaviour that encourages, because the cost of actions shapes how the whole game feels {{kvgKKRLil_8#2}} {{kvgKKRLil_8#10}}.
- Before you fix a bug or exploit, ask whether it is a "fountain" that opens new experiences worth keeping, and remove the ones that only make optimal play tedious {{kvCky6BbTuE#10}} {{kvCky6BbTuE#11}} {{QV57CYrIZow#12}} {{KLHJsJuJfYg#3}}.
- Loops with no brake are easy to design and hard to tune: give processes that spawn, reproduce or accumulate a cost with no free shortcut, and decide deliberately how much of a deep simulation's history to surface to the player {{jBhcHdV5HHo#13}} {{b2VkgcC1xRc#5}} {{FV_0rmqxbxw#15}} {{D8A9dpITLAU#0}} {{q7E1N-fJnrA#5}}.

## Why roguelikes are built from interacting systems

### The player must keep meeting new problems

Ask designers what a roguelike is for and several independent answers land in the same place: the player should keep facing situations they have not solved before. Brian Walker frames a successful roguelike as a chain of exciting situations, back to back, each with five ingredients: failure is very possible or the default, the player badly wants to win, the player identifies with the character rather than a data structure, skill rather than luck decides the outcome, and working out the answer is a fun puzzle. Chain those for an hour and the designer has done the job {{lfb6uehmrWo#1}}. Darren Grey puts the same idea in terms of Rogue itself: escalating threat paired with items and abilities that, used cleverly, let the player solve problems that are always new. That opportunity for ingenuity, and the feeling of being special and roguish when it pays off, is what Grey calls the real heart of the genre {{C2sm-z7Ag4U#3}}.

Neither of these is about content volume. They are about a supply of problems, and the supply has to come from somewhere other than a script. Dan Recht names the source: roguelikes have sets of interacting systems, and those systems force trade-offs, because you cannot be perfect at all of them at once. The trade-offs produce hard choices with large in-world consequences and strong feedback, which is why Recht sees the genre as a learning tool; if the choice goes wrong you simply try again {{FnJxkId-1eE#0}}.

### Where the new problems come from: combinations

George Moromisato explains why NetHack stays hard even though it is turn-based and, in the talk's phrase, an open book test. Chess has six unique pieces, so the current move is easy to read and its difficulty lies four or five moves ahead. NetHack is the opposite: it has so many items, verbs and monsters that even the immediate move is hard to evaluate. Its difficulty is breadth, not depth, and Moromisato ties that breadth to the game's richness and its emphasis on mastery {{QBZkvtGucBU#4}}. Years later, designing a modding system, Moromisato starts from the same place: combinatorial complexity, where items, monsters, effects and special attacks mix to produce new outcomes, is the feature they expect everyone to agree a roguelike needs, so any engine has to support it {{mES03vaZo3U#0}}.

Alice Lai, writing about Hades, draws the practical conclusion. A combo is anything where two or more elements produce a new and unexpected result, and combos should be counted as content alongside the areas, spells, monsters and biomes that designers normally list when they think about what a game contains. Learning them is also how players build a sense of mastery {{pXib0WTfLbI#2}}. Mike Cook goes a step further: designers control systems and rules, not the player's action space, which is always much larger than they can imagine. In a toy example, placing a campfire removes one move option and makes a fireball more attractive; the rule changed and the space of good moves shifted with it. That is why edge cases appear, and why Cook argues all game design is effectively generative design {{3daNITP9vBc#1}}.

### Emergence from simple rules

A strand of talks from outside the usual roguelike canon makes the same point from first principles: very small rules, interacting, produce structure the rules themselves do not describe. Emilia Lazer-Walker uses Conway's Game of Life, where gliders emerge from cell rules that say nothing about gliders, as the model for roguelike stories: here is how water works, here is how kobolds move, and the combination does things neither part does {{MNEG1sLSRcM#1}}. Benet Devereux reads biology the same way, taking Darwin's "tangled bank" as a statement that complex diversity comes from agents following simple principles, and turning it around into a design goal and a manifesto for procedural generation {{0iNW5AYYWWw#0}}. Nat Alison links L-systems and Tracery grammars to mathematical axioms, where a small set of rules (group theory, or counting plus addition and multiplication) generates an entire universe of structure {{5_UET-rUtGU#2}}, and points to Go, whose simple placement and capture rules carried thousands of years of theory and vocabulary {{5_UET-rUtGU#3}}. Lee Tusman says the attraction of Cosmic Crown and of lower-dimensional dungeons generally is that a very simple game can still produce surprising, emergent play, and the simplicity lets the player concentrate on understanding the system {{-e4gF_SKWPk#2}}.

The practitioners report the payoff. Joel Clark says that once systems interact in Blaseball, the complexity of outcome goes far beyond what scripting can reach, and the result surprises even the developers, which keeps players learning and interpreting {{zO05p-e57AQ#3}}. Casey Yano compares roguelikes to Super Smash Bros.: lots of chaotic levers whose combinations occasionally produce something the developer never planned for, such as laying an egg while you are a robot and hatching a robot dragon {{KYlq0Msu6hc#13}}. Todd Furmanski kept this even on the Atari 2600: put actors and items in a space without scripting, let each do its own thing, and interesting things happen {{l9eE4mT8xqI#3}}.

### Interaction as the core mechanic

Some designers go as far as naming interaction itself as the mechanic. Asked for Ultimate ADOM's core mechanic, summed up as "your dungeon, your rules", Thomas Biskup answered in one word, interaction, everything with everything, aiming at strategies the designers cannot foresee {{PTIsUSbQ0TA#15}}. Mark R Johnson's guiding rule for Ultima Ratio Regum is to make as many things relate to as many other things as possible, down to generated speech that refers to other parts of the world to compare, insult or praise them {{qh9vyoMviJI#9}}. Josh Ge, surveying how large roguelikes still revolve around a core mechanic (Mage Guild's mix-any-two-items alchemy, for instance), reports being told by a NetHack developer that NetHack's core mechanic is that if something seems like it should be possible, it probably is. Ge calls this almost an anti-core mechanic {{jviNpRGuCIU#11}}. Biskup's answer and the NetHack one make the same promise: the game is the interactions. The architecture section below shows how such a promise is kept in code.

### In practice

- Count interactions as content. When you plan what to build, list the combinations you expect players to discover next to the monsters and levels {{pXib0WTfLbI#2}}.
- Design rules, not outcomes. You set the rules; the player's action space will be larger than you think, so test what the rules make attractive rather than only what they allow {{3daNITP9vBc#1}}.
- Design for ingenuity. Give players combinable items and systems that offer clever escapes from seemingly impossible situations {{C2sm-z7Ag4U#12}}.
- Keep the clever options visible. Grey warns that some bigger games fill themselves with so much junk that players forget what is important or clever; signal the tools that matter {{C2sm-z7Ag4U#12}}.

## One rule set for player, monster and world

### The convergent principle: no special cases

Emergence depends on consistency. If the rules apply differently to the player, to monsters and to objects, interactions between them stop being predictable, and a player who cannot predict cannot plan. This is one of the clearest convergences in the talks.

Tarn Adams explains that every creature in Dwarf Fortress, the player included, is defined by the same kind of creature file and plays by the same rules: a dwarf or a human has a definition very similar to a kestrel's or an emu's. Even in a world that is not Earth, consistent and understandable rules let players anticipate what will happen, which is why the brothers think of roguelikes as narrative generators {{Zh6dnx9FM1g#12}}. Tyriq Plummer, citing Derek Yu's book on Spelunky, notes that of the Berlin Interpretation values that guided its design (the Berlin Interpretation is a community-written list of the features that define a roguelike), the one that makes Spelunky a roguelike platformer rather than a platformer with permadeath is a shared rule set for players, NPCs and items. Everything is equally hurt by spike traps and explosions and equally able to be picked up and thrown {{OVSi3NzA0KM#5}}.

droqen arrives at the same place from the player's side. When the player character is treated the same as monsters, the player feels embodied inside a simulated world rather than hovering above it as a special entity, as in The Sims {{6Whz8bvjAKo#1}}. In droqen's example game a teleporter sends anything that lands on it, blocks, monsters or the player, to the save room. The player reaches the save room the same way a monster does, so dropping monsters onto the teleporter makes the presumed safe hub dangerous. There are no fake walls or doors, only the game's rules {{6Whz8bvjAKo#2}}. Dustin Freeman calls the principle peership: the player character has no special status and is the protagonist only because they control it. Combined with bare-bones presentation, Freeman finds that players read rich inner lives into the other entities; in later ages of Spirit of the Ages the player can be a random entity such as a branch {{RYZSdPuvta8#3}}. Rich Wilson states the same rule for objects in immersive sims: every placed object should be a full representation of itself with all its systemic properties. A gas can placed anywhere can explode, be picked up and dropped, or fuel something; that consistency is what lets systems interact honestly across the whole world {{wtlLa7BGZcs#2}}.

> Every creature in the game is playing by the same rules. {{Zh6dnx9FM1g#12}}

### What happens when you take it seriously

Applied consistently, the principle has consequences that make for memorable games. Cataclysm: Dark Days Ahead's core tenet, as Jeremy Rose describes it, is that if something works in reality it should work in the game, so its 8,000-plus items carry mass, volume, material and temperature, most can be disassembled with the right tools, and temperatures follow seasons modelled on New England {{ziN33iMQVPo#1}}. In Caves of Qud every creature, even a non-bipedal one, has a full body and inventory, so Alexei Pepers describes combining the domination mutation with an item that grants a brain to inhabit a newly sentient door and look through its inventory. Pepers presents that moment of "what is this game capable of?" as a sign of how deep the systems go {{H5EU9_Gdu94#1}}. NetHack lets you genocide your own monster class, which kills you regardless of an amulet of life saving; Adeon, playing a dwarven Valkyrie, cannot wipe out all h's to remove mind flayers because their own character is one of them {{rIB0y_kwFuY#10}}. Zeno Rogue values the same consistency in space: HyperRogue's shops are part of the map rather than separate modes, which is what makes tricks like stealing from shops possible, whereas stairs violate it because nobody is sure whether enemies will follow {{wOlSws-OI2o#8}}.

### A world with its own business

Consistent rules extend naturally to a world that does not revolve around the player. Josh Ge describes Cogmind's robots as mostly busy with their own tasks, rebuilding walls and digging tunnels, rather than hunting the player; that living world is what lifts the game beyond bumping and fleeing {{9se2M27LuOs#17}}. In Caves of Qud factions fight over territory without you, and in one developer playthrough a goat-folk tribe clashes with another jungle tribe while the player, caught between them, has to run {{kDKQM27U-Nc#19}}. Bad News gives each character a daily routine across two periods, day and night; players who try a bar in the daytime find it locked and learn to use the wait command {{XFFyN10o87I#8}}. Jonathan Lessard's god game makes the player one of many simulated protector gods, created by communities and kept alive by their belief; believers pray, bargain and threaten to complain to their peers {{D8A9dpITLAU#5}}. The world goes on if you do nothing, and because your actions are limited and funded by belief, each one becomes an ethical choice about how much to meddle and who pays {{D8A9dpITLAU#8}}.

Max Kreminski explains what is at stake. Many games suffer from a solipsism problem: the world is bent around the player so thoroughly that anything they are not interacting with might as well not exist, a deeper version of frustum culling, where unseen objects are not rendered {{TvlZinAvpwg#5}}. Mining games add an extractive dynamic: the player's presence makes the world worse as ore, chests, NPCs and puzzles are used up, and infinite generation lets the player keep moving on rather than face the damage {{TvlZinAvpwg#9}}. Kreminski likes Animal Crossing's almost violent reaction to players who change the console clock, because it treats outside manipulation as an attack on the simulation's integrity {{TvlZinAvpwg#14}}. Two questions are left open. A world that changes without the player starting the change matters, but there is a trade-off between worlds that run only while the game is open and worlds that run alongside real time; Kreminski's Epitaph plays best in a background browser tab, yet closing the browser throws the world away {{TvlZinAvpwg#16}}. And they resist giving such a world an algorithmic score, because the world should exist for its own sake rather than as a number the player drives up, though they are curious about systems that measure unusual things {{TvlZinAvpwg#17}}.

### In practice

- Audit your code for player special cases. Every rule that checks "is this the player?" sets the player apart from the world instead of inside it; look for ones you can remove {{6Whz8bvjAKo#1}} {{RYZSdPuvta8#3}}.
- Define player, monsters and items from the same data, so that a trap, an explosion or a teleporter affects them all alike {{Zh6dnx9FM1g#12}} {{OVSi3NzA0KM#5}}.
- Never place a prop. Every object should carry its full systemic properties wherever it appears {{wtlLa7BGZcs#2}}.
- Give the world its own business: routines, jobs, faction conflicts that go on whether or not the player watches {{9se2M27LuOs#17}} {{kDKQM27U-Nc#19}}.
- Ask what the player's presence does to the world over time, and whether generation is hiding that from them {{TvlZinAvpwg#9}}.

## Choosing what to simulate: theme, emphasis and values

### Start from the setting's systems

Picking systems is picking what the game says. Brenda Romero designs dynamic traits for Empire of Sin by first listing every system in the setting (alcohol, bosses, crew, businesses, clients, citizens, police, feds) and studying where they intersect and collide. Only then can Romero see how a trait, such as a boss being a cheapskate, ripples through many systems and how strong or weak it will be {{_vf3HO3wn20#6}}. They add that choosing which system to emphasise and prioritise changes the whole nature of the game: a combat-forward game is very different from a pure empire builder, and mapping the systems shows the possibility space {{_vf3HO3wn20#7}}.

Jongwoo Kim found out what happens when the systems you borrow say the wrong thing. Kitfox wanted The Shrouded Isle to depict a spiritualist world of belief, virtue and moral decay, but most simulation references are materialist, about manipulating physical resources and growing economic value. Where they wanted virtues and vices they found questions of maximising value; where they wanted desperate control they found messages of prosperity {{oV5PjvcVky8#1}}. The game-jam version tracked food and shelter, which created survival pressure, but a full food bar implied prosperity and a cult leader would not manage those directly. The final game tracks five virtues (ignorance, fervour, discipline, penitence and obedience), raised or lowered by townspeople acting virtuously or sinfully, with the game ending if a bar stays below its threshold too long, plus each noble house's opinion, which depends on whose families you favour and whose members you sacrifice {{oV5PjvcVky8#2}}.

### Systems speak whether you mean them to

A recurring warning: a system carries a message even if you only added it out of habit. Ivy Melinda relays Jason Grinblat's finding that Caves of Qud's naive implementation of traditional roguelike heritage produced a "combat gravity well", a game more bloodthirsty than intended, because systems tell stories of their own {{db6S2lgTWmI#3}}. Emilia Lazer-Walker makes the general point: if a game's main verb is kill, players will resolve emergent setups with violence, as in an audience member's story of Richard Garriott's Ultima setup of sheep, town and dragon where players killed everything. Either embrace the carnage or give players other verbs that invite the behaviour you want {{MNEG1sLSRcM#12}}. Darren Grey notes that many big roguelikes copy Rogue's mechanics, such as the hunger clock, without thinking, and wonders why NetHack and ADOM bother with hunger beyond Rogue having had it {{C2sm-z7Ag4U#16}}.

The pull runs both ways, and the talks do not agree on which side should win. Brian Walker cut a gag from Brogue in which a zombie's gas made you vomit and each vomit drained food, because the food clock served an important purpose and the joke broke it: let go of the metaphor when there is a gameplay reason to {{lfb6uehmrWo#17}}. Michael Brough describes the reverse drift. When Vancian spell memorisation became a mechanic, players wanted two copies of the same spell, which makes no sense in the fiction, and the system slid towards abstract mana points: flexible, popular, and stripped of flavour {{zDKheM-E45I#5}}. Walker gave up flavour to protect a system; Brough shows that giving up flavour step by step for convenience can leave nothing distinctive. Some choices are ethical rather than aesthetic. Xalavier Nelson's team refused to make organ value in Space Warlord Organ Trading Simulator depend on size, because that would have meant developers deciding whose bodies are healthier or better, edging towards what Nelson calls eugenics the video game; they narrowed the game to about 20 organs and left size out of the value {{SdQxLThAbs8#2}}.

### Constructive routes from theme to system

Ludipe lays out two ways to build a game around a theme, using pacifist roguelikes as the case. The cheap one is a reskin: keep an existing combat system and replace its wrapper, turning Rift Wizard's spell duels into ballroom gossip where damage becomes reputation or affection. It is not always possible, but it often works and feels fresh, even though it is the same game in new wrapping {{pYtlU9wGopo#3}}. The harder route is to derive new systems from the theme's fantasy, as Insurmountable does with mini-systems about being a mountain climber. Each iteration is harder, more ideas are discarded and it takes longer, but it reaches more interesting situations {{pYtlU9wGopo#4}}.

Science is one rich source for that second route. Benet Devereux turns real organism strategies into mechanics by abstraction: ask what resources matter to each actor, what senses they use and how they interact, and make sure the result is a meaningful choice rather than a button to keep pressing. Devereux used asknature.org's taxonomy of strategies, written for engineers, as an idea generator {{0iNW5AYYWWw#2}}. They also warn that many biological strategies depend on form and material properties that would need a rich physics engine, and point instead to resource constraints and collective behaviour as promising {{0iNW5AYYWWw#6}}. Philomena Schwab describes how Niche turned the subtopics of population genetics, such as mutation, into mechanics; the result made a good game, found a built-in audience, and is used in schools though it was never meant to be educational {{wLLDPz8PnQg#8}}.

The same act can mean different things depending on what it serves. Everest Pipkin notes that digging in games usually gamifies the satisfaction of labour without its bodily exhaustion, digging without calluses {{VwZS0MQSpaU#0}}, and that although it plays the same nearly everywhere (equip a tool, click a spot), its goals vary so much that it is a mechanic rather than a genre {{VwZS0MQSpaU#1}}. Treasure-seeking digging usually leaves no mark: soil re-smooths, treasure regenerates, dig spots move each day {{VwZS0MQSpaU#6}}. Terrain-sculpting games such as Animal Crossing: New Horizons instead let you reshape the world and then watch residents and creatures live on the surface you built {{VwZS0MQSpaU#7}}. Whether the system's effects persist is part of what it says.

Even one small interaction can change what everything means. Younès Rabii added a pick-up action to Tea Garden, where the armless character places items on its head, and players began seeing previously meaningless flowers as hats; an orange one became a cowboy hat and a favourite {{s6Kxh_Zyiqs#3}}. When flowers could be placed on the ground, players arranged them and read some as letters. Rabii's theory is that each new interaction gives players more blanks to fill, so the game and player build a visual language together {{s6Kxh_Zyiqs#4}}. Matthew Davis points to an under-explored example in FTL: its crew and power micromanagement, which Davis considers its coolest and least-imitated element, more than the text events others copied, and wants successors to twist ideas aggressively into something new {{rNi5nO5yqLs#5}}. Jeff Lait sums up why this matters in a genre whose fonts and visuals are rarely designed by the author: the art should live in the code and the game systems {{LQPGmxytTws#2}}.

> The art should live in the code of the game. The game systems is where the art should live. {{LQPGmxytTws#2}}

### Questions to ask of each system

- What does it reward? If the main verb is kill, expect killing {{MNEG1sLSRcM#12}} {{db6S2lgTWmI#3}}.
- What does it say? A full food bar says prosperity; check whether that is your theme {{oV5PjvcVky8#2}}.
- What value judgement does it encode, and are you qualified to make it {{SdQxLThAbs8#2}}?
- Why is it here? If the answer is "Rogue had it", find a better one or cut it {{C2sm-z7Ag4U#16}}.
- When flavour and function conflict, which are you trading away, and will the trade leave anything distinctive {{lfb6uehmrWo#17}} {{zDKheM-E45I#5}}?

## How many pieces: dense interactions versus sprawling content

### Two schools

If roguelikes run on interactions, how many things should interact? The talks split into two camps. One says few, densely connected. Eric Billingsley argues that fewer elements force you to tune how every system interacts, producing a more focused, cohesive and learnable game than one with a thousand loosely connected things, where you will not consider all the combinations and some parts feel separate. Reaching roguelike emergence with fewer elements is all the more striking, and simpler games are faster to make {{3S6uRMCdvNs#19}}. Michael Brough agrees, with a specific reason: effects keyed to a particular enemy type only matter when there are few types. Rogue has about one monster per letter; 868-HACK has four enemy types, so an ability that hits all Virus enemies affects roughly a quarter of them, and Cinco Paus has five. With hundreds of types, systematic rules are the right choice instead {{X4be22WoVgg#7}}. Dan Norder says adding features to Chase dilutes the minimalist purity that makes it addictive and helped it last for generations {{ohH6LBM2EiU#11}}, and Lee Tusman's One Dimensional Dungeon collapses Rogue to a line while keeping recognisable systems such as hallucinating after hitting a toad and blindness from a floating eye {{-e4gF_SKWPk#7}}.

The other camp is breadth. NetHack's difficulty, as Moromisato describes it in the first section, comes precisely from the number of items, verbs and monsters {{QBZkvtGucBU#4}}. Casey Yano describes Slay the Spire dropping a three-slot relic limit in favour of unlimited, Binding of Isaac-style accumulation, where items trigger off other items in cascading chaos. Discarding excess relics felt bad, the team enjoyed designing many relics, and the change playtested much better over more than a year {{KYlq0Msu6hc#3}}.

The choice is not about which is right but about what kind of interaction you want. Dense, small sets support type-specific exceptions and let players learn the whole system; broad sets support systematic rules and long-term mastery of a huge space. Johnathon Pagnutti adds a third consideration: detailed mechanics can exist without mattering. In Fire Emblem: Three Houses, stats barely drive character performance unless difficulty is very high, so mechanics should be judged against the audience and the intended experience. Pagnutti personally finds that simple yes/no probabilities can produce very dynamic play, while acknowledging a tradition of beautiful complex systems {{DbZe8rWviS0#5}}.

### Exceptions and twists: Brough's craft

Brough's 2021 talk gives concrete technique for the dense school. First, magic lives in one-off exceptions. In Rogue, the wand of cold does not harm the yeti, and the story of a cold-adapted beast happens in the player's head; a fully logical, systematic magic system with elemental resistance percentages starts to feel like science. Generalised systems are good software engineering, but the design does not have to mirror the code {{X4be22WoVgg#5}}. Second, the sweet spot is several things that follow a rule and exactly one that breaks it. With a single exception players accept it as special; add a second and they infer a more general rule {{X4be22WoVgg#6}}.

Third, value effects that twist a situation rather than make it strictly better or worse. Rogue's wand of polymorph tends to make weak enemies stronger and strong ones weaker, and it combos with type-specific effects: facing a yeti with only a wand of cold, polymorph it first so the cold works. Brough builds whole games around such elements so these combos come up often {{X4be22WoVgg#13}}. Small numbers keep twists cheap: 868-HACK's screen-wide polymorph turns each enemy into one of the three other types, all of comparable strength, so it changes abilities rather than the strength of the situation and stays predictable {{X4be22WoVgg#14}}. Fourth, tame variance with several small fixes. 868-HACK's warp is a random teleport that only lands on an enemy-occupied space, kills that enemy and does not end the turn. Choosing among a few enemy spaces instead of hundreds of tiles makes it predictable, the kill is a bonus, and the free action gives a chance to respond. Brough says any one of these fixes would make it acceptable; all three make it strong {{X4be22WoVgg#12}}.

### Variety from recombination

If you cannot or do not want to generate interesting structure, you can get variety by making the pieces rich. Tom Francis's rule for Heat Signature is: if you can't make the shuffle interesting, make more interesting cards. Card games are not made interesting by smarter shuffles but by cards that carry meaning and new rules, and Francis applies this to a level generator that can only shuffle walls around {{3vSCncV5hkI#4}}. The "cards" come in three kinds, layered on top of shuffled levels: player tools, enemy traits such as shields and armour or ways to detect and push back against the player, and global filters {{3vSCncV5hkI#5}}. A filter is a global change that warps the player's experience of the same content, so the same kind of level plays differently {{3vSCncV5hkI#9}}. Francis's summary of shuffle-friendly design: make elements that produce something interesting merely by being rearranged, and design tools whose constraints and freedoms relate closely to what gets shuffled. Tools, traits and filters are cheap to vary and combine with each other and with the levels, so the levels themselves can stay boring {{3vSCncV5hkI#13}}.

> If you can't make the shuffle interesting, make more interesting cards. {{3vSCncV5hkI#4}}

Tim Keenan shows how to make runs distinct from a shared pool. Either synergies steer players to different picks, as in Slay the Spire, or each run draws only a subset: Below the Crown has five board hazards but any one run gets only two. Duskers instead lets every problem happen in any run. Limiting the subset makes runs more distinct from each other at the cost of variety within a run {{R8DSq8qSJ44@30756#6}}.

### Layers, and active over passive

Evan Debenham describes Shattered Pixel Dungeon as a stack of layers that are each simple, core tap-to-act play, consumables and equipment, hero and enemy abilities, level structure and hazards, but that interact to create depth. Leaving the core layers mostly untouched is why it still feels like Pixel Dungeon: early on you tap a simple enemy, late on you turn a staff of fireblast on a boss with its own mechanics, but the core is unchanged {{NMWQIdfCgQg#12}}. Debenham also argues that active effects that change the player's decisions are generally much more fun than passive bonuses like +1 damage, though some simple passives are still needed because too much complexity confuses {{NMWQIdfCgQg#11}}.

Breadth can go wrong when the pieces do not fit together. John Harris describes Shiren 4 and 5's day/night system, which replaces every monster at night with much stronger ones and turns play into stealth where items work poorly. Because the series is about creative application of tools, fewer strategies work at night, and players who like one mode hate the other. Harris calls it an interesting experiment; it was cut from Shiren 6 {{0MdNwIHwXy0#13}}.

### In practice

- Choose a density deliberately. Few elements let you tune every interaction and support one-off exceptions; many elements call for systematic rules {{3S6uRMCdvNs#19}} {{X4be22WoVgg#7}}.
- Allow one exception per rule, not two {{X4be22WoVgg#6}}.
- Prefer effects that change the situation or the player's decision over flat bonuses {{X4be22WoVgg#13}} {{NMWQIdfCgQg#11}}.
- When an effect is too random, constrain where it lands, attach a bonus, or give the player a chance to respond {{X4be22WoVgg#12}}.
- Get variety from rich, recombinable pieces (tools, enemy traits, global filters) and consider limiting each run to a subset of them {{3vSCncV5hkI#13}} {{R8DSq8qSJ44@30756#6}}.
- Watch for whole modes that do not fit with the rest of the game, and for complexity the audience will never feel {{0MdNwIHwXy0#13}} {{DbZe8rWviS0#5}}.

## Uncertainty: randomness, hidden information and the simulated dice

### Randomness that stands for something

In a systemic game, randomness works best when it is tied to the simulation. Aaron A. Reed shows this in the original Oregon Trail, which drew from a pool of about 20 random events whose presentation and probability depended on miles travelled. A weather event prints cold weather and checks for cold-weather gear past roughly 950 miles, the Rockies, but rain before that. The chance of a rider attack is a single BASIC equation over mileage: low in Missouri, peaking on the open plains, falling towards zero beyond the Rockies. The game never shows a landmark, yet geography is encoded in how events appear {{CE7o3GGif30#3}}. Reed's general principle is randomness constrained by a plausibility space informed by a simulation, balancing the predictable and the unpredictable and producing a little universe the designer cannot fully control {{CE7o3GGif30#4}}.

Nic Tringali gives the other half of the argument. Dice represent true uncertainty that nobody, the game included, knows yet, and they stand in for everything the game cannot or does not want to simulate. When an orc throws the ball in Blood Bowl, wind, sweat, crowd distractions and other players' movement all exist in the fiction, but they lie outside the player's knowledge and are captured in one roll {{R8DSq8qSJ44@17074#7}}. Read together, Reed and Tringali give a design rule: decide what you simulate explicitly, and let a random draw, shaped by the simulated state, stand for the rest.

Xalavier Nelson's experience pushes back. In Space Warlord Organ Trading Simulator the team tied each stock to overall market movement, the tickers converged, and both internal and external QA called it unrealistic and worse to play. The more they tried to make the market make sense, the less it worked; when they embraced true randomness, players believed it {{SdQxLThAbs8#5}}. So structure is not always more convincing. What players find plausible depends on what they believe about the thing simulated: weather follows geography, but a market, in Nelson's account, rests on human faith and pattern-finding in noise.

### Sources of uncertainty

Randomness is only one source. Shawn Main offers what they call a half-formed list: physical skill, an opponent, hidden information (which tests prediction, memory and observation), delayed narrative consequences, complex systems where one action cascades as in a match-3 game, and overt randomness {{UZ7XqQYDM7o#12}}. Andrea Roberts notes that RNGs are usually constrained to simulate a die roll, a bag pull or a shuffle, and asks what else could serve: gambling, scientific and climate models, human psychology, genetic mutation, divination such as cleromancy, and, an audience member added, other players. Each brings a different texture and feeling {{2BHfpAF76J8#6}}. Michael Toy, Glenn Wichman and Ken Arnold's Rogue panel offers a historical aside: Arnold made it a rule that every Rogue release would break Rog-O-Matic, the AI built to play Rogue, using quirks such as hallucination that added unpredictability without changing core play {{IaB7iQhts_c#8}}.

Hidden information alone can change a genre. Jonathan Lessard describes how adding Rogue's limited visibility to chess's one-hit captures turned Chogue into survival horror: walking into a dark room to see your queen taken felt horrible in an engaging way {{l1YEJBKehAY#8}}. Tringali generalises: decks and dice have no inherent sense of past or future. Their effect depends on how players read them, and the same effect could come from rolling dice in advance and storing the results. What matters is what the player knows and does not know {{R8DSq8qSJ44@17074#11}}.

Scale is another source. Nick Cummings points out that baseball runs on nested time scales, season, game, inning, half-inning, at-bat, about 2,430 major league games a season, repeated every year, and that sheer volume of repeated play leaves huge room for strange outcomes {{R8DSq8qSJ44@2704#0}}. And uncertainty can be scheduled rather than left to chance. Cezar Capacle's "structured randomness" uses explicit play procedures, even flowcharts (frame the scene, decide the complication, choose an approach, roll, conclude, repeat), to tell players when to use oracles and complications. The procedures guide mechanics, not story beats, and in Capacle's experience produce freer, more diverse stories and a game you can trust {{NklQJfhZT3k#9}}.

Small, well-placed randomness can also simply be charming. On multi-user Unix systems NetHack delivered real email as a scroll brought by a mail daemon; Keni added random fake mail to the Amiga port to keep that fun interruption, and browser NetHack has since repurposed it for messages between players {{9V_CkJ66uJQ#5}}.

### In practice

- Decide where uncertainty enters: skill, opponents, hidden information, delayed consequences, cascading systems or overt dice {{UZ7XqQYDM7o#12}}.
- Make random draws depend on simulated state, as Oregon Trail's events depend on mileage, so randomness tells the player something about the world {{CE7o3GGif30#3}} {{CE7o3GGif30#4}}.
- Use a roll for what you choose not to simulate, and be clear with yourself what that is {{R8DSq8qSJ44@17074#7}}.
- Test plausibility with players rather than assuming structure looks real; Nelson's market felt truer once it was random {{SdQxLThAbs8#5}}.
- Consider hiding information instead of adding dice; visibility rules alone can change the genre of the experience {{l1YEJBKehAY#8}} {{R8DSq8qSJ44@17074#11}}.
- Look beyond dice and decks for new textures of chance {{2BHfpAF76J8#6}}.

## Architectures that let everything interact

The sections so far are about what to build. This one and the three after it are about building it so that the pieces combine. The promise of a systemic roguelike is that anything can affect anything, and the breadth described at the start of the chapter is the goal. The arithmetic is against you. With a hundred kinds of object, there are thousands of pairs, and every new item adds another hundred cases. If you write each interaction by hand, two things happen: the code gets brittle, and you stop adding content because every addition costs so much {{wtlLa7BGZcs#1}} {{QBZkvtGucBU#8}}. The talks reach the same answer from several directions. Don't write rules for pairs of objects. Give objects properties, and write rules for the properties.

### Code properties, not pairs

George Moromisato traces this back to NetHack. The game's reputation that "the dev team thinks of everything" rests on combinations like wielding a cockatrice corpse with or without gloves, each of which someone had to decide and code. What makes this possible "in a human lifetime", in Moromisato's account, is that items are grouped by material (metal, leather, wood) and monsters by attributes like fire resistance. Interactions are then written between attributes rather than between every pair of things. The edge cases are still a lot of work, but there are far fewer of them. Moromisato adopted the approach for their own game, Transcendence {{QBZkvtGucBU#8}}.

In a later talk on modding Moromisato takes the idea a step further, with a model of how an engine like Dwarf Fortress might work. They say this is an illustration, not the actual implementation. The engine defines forces (piercing, bludgeoning, heat) and materials, and it decides what happens when a force meets a material. Items and monsters are then defined only in those terms: a fireball does heat damage, a magic creature is made of some material, and the engine works out the rest. Because content is just data, modders need no C++, and Moromisato notes that very sophisticated Dwarf Fortress mods exist purely as data files {{mES03vaZo3U#3}}.

Rich Wilson, whose rule that every placed object carries its full systemic properties appears in the section on one rule set, describes the same move as immersive sims make it, drawing on a GDC talk by Randy and Harvey Smith from around 2004. Hand-coding every pairwise interaction is brittle and discourages new content. Instead, objects emit stimuli and other objects respond to stimuli. A flaming arrow and a candle both produce fire; a torch and an oil puddle both respond to fire. When a guard is shot and drops their torch into a puddle of oil, the oil ignites because the systems connect, not because a designer placed a trigger volume there {{wtlLa7BGZcs#1}}.

> Distill that stuff down into this sort of stimuli that they produce and then things that can respond to stimuli. {{wtlLa7BGZcs#1}}

### Components and bodies

An entity component system (ECS) applies the same thinking to the code itself. An entity is little more than an ID; its abilities come from the components attached to it. Thomas Biskup, who names interaction as Ultimate ADOM's core mechanic, argues that once features are pulled out into components instead of hard-coded, they plug together "like Lego pieces", and the combinations produce what Biskup calls a combinatorial explosion of possibilities. For Ultimate ADOM they expect players to invent strategies the developers never thought of, which a less flexible system would not allow {{fGLJC5UY2o4#4}}.

The most concrete example is the body. In Ultimate ADOM a hand grants the ability to handle doors and to fight in melee. The door system only asks whether an entity can handle doors. So a character whose hands are hacked off can no longer open or close doors, and a creature with six arms gets six melee attacks, without either case being written as special code. A humanoid hand extends the generic hand with a slot that takes a weapon or shield, and the hand's volume decides how likely an amputation is to remove it {{fGLJC5UY2o4#14}}. A smaller example from the same system: items have a size relative to their size class, so a fire giant wields a hugely sized longsword with its own damage and weight, not the medium one an orc would use {{fGLJC5UY2o4#12}}.

Liza Knipscher reaches the same design from creature simulation. Bioloom gives each organism its functions from the body parts it spawned with. Legs allow movement only if they reach the ground; leaves enable photosynthesis; a creature with a trunk or root body is stationary; one with no eyes, mouth or head has no brain and wanders; one with no nose or mouth that cannot breathe through its skin suffocates {{jBhcHdV5HHo#5}}. Knipscher's design reason is that a creature sim is more satisfying when appearance matters to function. In Wobbledogs body shape visibly affects movement: a four-legged dog moves easily, one with a leg in front and one behind struggles. They suggest asking of each part whether it does something: do antlers defend, does fur camouflage, do legs reach the ground? That is what makes evolution meaningful {{jBhcHdV5HHo#10}}.

Biskup and Knipscher are describing one pattern at different scales. A capability belongs to a part, the system asks about the capability, never about the creature type, and losing or gaining parts changes behaviour for free.

### Rules that compose

Scott Kovach comes at the problem from the programming side. They argue that roguelikes should aim for unexpected interactions between rule systems, and build an example one layer at a time: movement; then impassable water and lava; then boots of flying; then, written by a separate developer, a material and combustion simulation that makes the boots cloth. The result is a player who falls into the lava when their boots burn. Kovach calls it the kind of funny story games should produce, and points out that in conventional code each new layer forces edits to the old ones {{s9Wyn9BJJI4#3}}. The alternative Kovach proposes is a rule system based on relational programming. Each fact is written independently: boots are cloth, lava tiles have temperature 100, and a worn item combusts when the temperature at its wearer's position exceeds its combustion point. Further rules act as event handlers: a combusting worn item is unequipped and destroyed. A fact produced on a rule's right-hand side is created like an event, and any rule that mentions that fact on its left-hand side responds to it {{s9Wyn9BJJI4#8}}. Different authors can add rules without touching each other's code.

### Data that others can extend

The payoff of these architectures is that content becomes data, and data can be extended by people who are not programmers. Kate Compton points to The Sims 1, where every object is a bundle of data: images, a footprint, the needs it advertises, an animation and the needs it actually satisfies. Expansions added objects without changing or debugging code. Players found uses the designers never expected: one user-made smoking table reused the chess table and advertised that it would reduce loneliness without actually doing so {{isB82kORimk#7}}.

Tarn Adams shows the same principle applied to social structure. Dwarf Fortress's villain plots are networks of agents: a necromancer, an apprentice, a ruler and so on. Because characters die constantly, the network cannot depend on a global analysis. Each node repairs itself with local checks. If the necromancer dies, the apprentice checks whether immortality is still on offer and wanders off if not, but the ruler still hates their targets, so the war and murders go on. If the apprentice dies, the necromancer looks for other known agents or goes up the chain. Adams compares this to entity-component thinking and says the local approach keeps it fast and simple, and lets it fail sometimes {{4-7TtPX5uhg#11}}. Because the relationships and missions are generalised data (loyalty and trust used in dice rolls, a default mission to expand the network), the same system lets the player send companions on missions and become a villain, or some other kind of actor, at little extra cost. The main new work is an interface to give goals; the missions run through the existing army controller, so companions can do anything a dwarf squad can {{4-7TtPX5uhg#21}}.

Aaron A. Reed finds the idea already present in the text adventure The Hobbit. It checks for goal states, such as the dragon being dead, rather than for a specific solution. Because objects had weight, size and fragility and other characters acted on their own, there were many ways to reach a goal: kill the dragon yourself, lead another character to do it, or let a wandering monster do it. Companions could carry you out of the goblin tunnels {{CE7o3GGif30#9}}. If you test the world's state instead of the player's sequence of actions, the systems can find routes you did not write.

### In practice: patterns and their costs

- **Shared attributes** (materials, resistances). Interactions are written once per attribute, not per object pair. The cost: edge cases remain and still need individual decisions {{QBZkvtGucBU#8}}.
- **Forces on materials, defined in data.** Content and modding move into data files. The cost: the engine is complex and may take years to build, and its outcomes get harder to predict, so expect emergent bugs as well as emergent behaviour {{mES03vaZo3U#3}} {{mES03vaZo3U#4}}.
- **Stimulus and response.** Objects emit and react, so new objects join existing chains instead of needing hand-coded interactions with every other object {{wtlLa7BGZcs#1}}.
- **Components and body parts.** Capabilities come from parts, so injuries, grafts and odd anatomies work without special code. The cost: every system has to ask about a capability, never about a creature type {{fGLJC5UY2o4#14}} {{jBhcHdV5HHo#5}}.
- **Independent rules with event facts.** Separate authors' rules compose without editing old code. The cost: you need a rule engine, and you need to get used to thinking relationally {{s9Wyn9BJJI4#8}}.
- **Local repair in agent networks.** This is robust against constant death and cheap to run. The cost: results are sometimes wrong, and you have to accept that {{4-7TtPX5uhg#11}}.
- **Goal states, not scripted solutions.** Players and the simulation find their own routes. The cost: you lose control over how a goal is reached {{CE7o3GGif30#9}}.

## Terrain, materials and physical simulation

The cheapest way to put interacting systems in front of a player is to build them into the ground. Brian Walker makes the strongest version of the claim: rich terrain is fundamentally more interesting than monsters. It explains itself, it makes space evocative, and it supports plans the player can feel good about. Dry grass obviously burns, while a goblin shaman's abilities are a mystery until you learn them. Walker points to dungeon-crawl films like Indiana Jones, where most of the danger comes from spikes, traps, floods and burning vegetation, and terrain often settles fights with monsters. Throwing an incendiary dart to burn grass towards an acid mound is a "MacGyver moment", more exciting than standing aside while monsters fight each other {{lfb6uehmrWo#13}}.

### Terrain as the player's weapon

Several talks describe the same player experience. Darren Grey, asked which game makes you feel most like a rogue, names Brogue, because it lets you be powerful without feeling powerful: you lure monsters into lava, set fields on fire and trigger gas traps while your character stays weak. The power comes from outside sources, not from your own strength, and that makes you feel clever {{C2sm-z7Ag4U#15}}. A commentator on a Brogue exhibition run pointed out the same features to viewers: open caverns full of foliage that incendiary darts can ignite, and deep water you can wade into but shouldn't, because your items float away and eels attack {{zlJNIJOog9M#2}}.

Simulated systems become tactics when they chain. Jeremy Rose's account of Cataclysm gives a full chain. Winter freezes your drinks. If you thaw them by lighting a fire on a flammable floor instead of in an oven or container, the house burns. The beams collapse loudly, and the noise draws zombies. So a deliberate house fire becomes a distraction while you loot somewhere else {{ziN33iMQVPo#2}}. Caves of Qud has a shorter chain with a sting in it. Nick DeCapua warns that a burning character who jumps into water may be hot enough to turn it into steam, which can be deadlier than the fire. The fire also spreads to trees {{kDKQM27U-Nc#20}}. Thomas Biskup demonstrates Ultimate ADOM's spells changing terrain in the same spirit. An ice storm freezes water on the floor so monsters slip, stun or slow; a fireball melts it again; and casting fireball too often in a small room wears down the walls until other monsters can reach you {{PTIsUSbQ0TA#11}}. Biskup presents that last effect as an unintended consequence of a world that is fully defined rather than decoration.

### Brogue's toolkit: spreading, diffusion and gravity

Walker's techniques show how much you can do with a few general mechanics. Brogue uses one mechanic, terrain spreading over other terrain, for fire spreading, bridges collapsing, walkways extending, lakes receding, vegetation growing and tunnels appearing. Because the effect is so common, terrain feels alive {{lfb6uehmrWo#14}}. Gas is a quantity stored on each tile. Every turn each tile averages its gas with its eight neighbours, and that simple formula produces round clouds even on a square grid {{lfb6uehmrWo#12}}.

In a later talk Walker adds gravity, which breaks the symmetry between up and down in a top-down roguelike. With gravity, vines can spread, gases can be buoyant or heavy, liquids can fall and evaporate, and terrain can emit or absorb gases and liquids. Doors hold them back until opened, and lava plus water makes steam. Walker describes a Brogue-style machine that floods lava with water vents to fill a room with steam, where whether you survive depends on the room's shape. Structural support is easy to compute and easy to see, so explosions can cut platforms loose and they collapse, much as caves fall in in Dwarf Fortress {{Uo9-IcHhq_w#15}}.

### Cellular automata for materials

A cellular automaton (CA) is a grid in which each cell updates from its own state and its neighbours' by fixed rules. It is the standard engine for this kind of simulation. Scott Anderson notes that Minecraft and Dwarf Fortress use CAs for fluids and Brogue for gases and fire, usually tracking how full each cell is. Dwarf Fortress adds flood-fill rules that try to keep water level, and the "slow water" technique uses two states, one moving left and one moving right {{R8DSq8qSJ44@15170#1}}. Ian Holmes widens the lineage: SimCity's interacting CA layers (studied in a PhD thesis), Dwarf Fortress, Minecraft and Caves of Qud's walls with melting points, alongside scientific models of fire, crystal growth and diffusion {{b2VkgcC1xRc#2}}.

Anderson's talk is specific enough to build a falling-sand system from:

- **Six base movement rules.** Games with hundreds of materials are mostly variations on empty, solid, falling, powder, liquid and gas. Empty matters because most rules move things into it. Solids block. Falling elements drop. Powders also slide diagonally when blocked. Liquids also slide sideways when the whole row below is full. Anderson's gas is an inverted powder rule that randomly dissipates. They note that Noita and The Powder Toy each have around 400 elements, many of them variations on these rules {{R8DSq8qSJ44@15170#8}}.
- **"Alchemy" for interactions.** A cell changes state based on the types of its neighbours. That covers fire, acid, ice, snow and plants. Fire moves randomly upward, ignites flammable neighbours and turns into smoke. Plants grow by consuming water. Acid destroys neighbours. Ice and snow freeze water. Electricity borrows the head, tail and conductor states of the Wireworld automaton, and fire borrows from the forest-fire automaton. Creatures such as ants and flies can move randomly and consume food the way fire consumes fuel {{R8DSq8qSJ44@15170#7}}.
- **Probabilities as the tuning knobs.** Rules decide by random percentage chance, and cells can store extra state such as temperature or lifetime. Changing how fast things burn or freeze, or whether sand falls left or right, changes the system's behaviour dramatically. Anderson says emergent behaviour is often not what you want, so they tune these probabilities by hand and playtest, like any game-feel work {{R8DSq8qSJ44@15170#5}}.
- **Update order.** Because the whole grid updates every frame, the order you visit cells decides how far material moves per tick. A top-down pass would carry a grain of sand all the way to the floor in one update, so falling material is updated bottom-up, and rising gases need an inverted, top-down pass. The Margolus scheme, which updates the grid in non-overlapping blocks, avoids overlaps within one pass and alternates its offset between passes to reduce directional bias {{R8DSq8qSJ44@15170#3}} {{R8DSq8qSJ44@15170#5}}.
- **Density by swapping.** To make sand sink in water, treat the liquid as empty space for heavier elements and swap the two cells. Some games build a full density ordering, but swapping is enough; lighter elements can float the same way {{R8DSq8qSJ44@15170#6}}.

Holmes adds that the cells need not be deterministic or even meaningful one by one. In Holmes's predator-prey "zookeeper" prototype, particles moved almost like Brownian motion, "not really going anywhere", yet a rock-paper-scissors ecology emerged at the large scale, and the player's job was to build enclosures to keep the species from wiping each other out. Later versions added particle fluids, permeable membranes and cells running small programs, rebuilt on a C virtual machine for speed {{b2VkgcC1xRc#3}}. Holmes's touchstone is Exile, a metroidvania that combined a curated procedural map (to fit a 32K memory limit) with Newtonian physics and creatures that interacted with each other and with mass and viscosity {{b2VkgcC1xRc#0}}.

Turn-based games have a timing advantage here. As Todd Furmanski put it in a 2020 preview panel, a player deciding a move takes an eternity in computer time, and that gap can be spent simulating weather, day and night, temperature and material chemistry. Furmanski's goal is a chemical simulation for every material, so that a fireball melts snow, singes cloth and weakens metal as well as hurting the monster. They admit that simulating everything all the time is still hard {{tmrYfnMrifw#7}}.

The same flood-fill logic extends beyond matter. In Q&A, Albert Ford agreed that field-of-view and pathfinding algorithms could model hearing, since a pathfinder is essentially a directed flood fill and sound similarly fades with distance {{y1zkrTcNJbc#7}}. Nick Bush, discussing their word-processor roguelike, imagines a deformable map. If the text reflowed, players could shift the level at will and trap enemies on hostile text, and they would have to predict the reflow the way Tetris and match-three players predict how the board settles. Bush left reflow out, partly because the game was built by hand with rot.js {{R8DSq8qSJ44@1660#6}}.

### When simulation stops paying

Noita is the extreme case: every pixel is a material that can react with others (fire ignites wood, water turns to steam and puts out fire), and that falling-sand simulation was the founding idea of the game {{huMHQGr8PgM#0}}. Arvi Teikari's account of making it is the clearest warning in the talks. First, destruction. Letting players destroy everything is the easiest way to show off a physics simulation, and it quickly becomes boring, because once everything is destroyed there is nothing left to see. In the first arena prototype, turning everything into acid was the most fun way to play and also the most boring, leaving each level a melted heap. Unlimited destruction is either a constant danger to the player or a way to trivialise the game {{huMHQGr8PgM#7}}. Second, most plausible reactions do not turn into decisions. Ice freezing, sand pouring and fungal soil sprouting are interesting in themselves, but only a small set of reactions were immediately useful for gameplay. Wood burning is easy to make into a mechanic; for the rest, the team constantly had to ask how players would see the effects and make meaningful use of them {{huMHQGr8PgM#8}}.

Rich Wilson sees this as where the field is heading. As fine-grained simulation of the kind in Noita and Teardown becomes feasible, so that everything wooden burns and not just one box, Wilson calls it a "Moore's law inevitability", the hard part moves to design: working out what to do with a world where everything responds {{wtlLa7BGZcs#16}}. Teikari is reporting from the far side of that shift, and their answer is that most of the simulation's output needs design work before it becomes play.

### In practice

- Simulate the properties that are self-explanatory and that support plans, such as burning, flooding, collapse and gas. Walker's argument for terrain over monsters depends on the player being able to read the property at a glance {{lfb6uehmrWo#13}}.
- Prefer a few general mechanics with many uses (spreading terrain, averaged diffusion, gravity) to many single-purpose effects {{lfb6uehmrWo#14}} {{Uo9-IcHhq_w#15}}.
- For each reaction you simulate, ask Teikari's question: how will the player see this, and what decision does it create {{huMHQGr8PgM#8}}?
- Limit destruction so the world stays worth looking at and stays a threat {{huMHQGr8PgM#7}}.
- Expect to tune probabilities and update order by hand; emergent output is not automatically the output you want {{R8DSq8qSJ44@15170#5}}.

## Items, magic and hazards built to combine

An item, spell or trap justifies its place by what it does in combination with everything else. Used alone, it is a number. Connected to other systems, it is a problem the player can solve in more than one way. John Harris puts the player's side of this in an account of the Mystery Dungeon games: their essence is the creative application of every trick you have found, against monsters you can barely escape {{0MdNwIHwXy0#2}}. Reed Lockwood gives the designer's side for traps. The most important principle, they say, is to tie traps into the game's other systems: combat, monsters, even a religion system, where a trap might draw a deity's attention {{ymgmhtkRLyY#7}}.

### What happens when an element stands alone

Nick McConnell's experience maintaining Angband shows the failure. Angband's traps degenerated into a "press button not to die" check once every character could get easy trap detection early in the game. The traps had no other connection, so detection cancelled them, and the team had to rework the system to make traps interesting again {{LaOcggk7U88#7}}. McConnell's wider point is that mechanics in a long-lived game ossify. Some once worked and slowly decayed; others never worked and nobody objected. An element with only one hook is easy to neutralise with a single counter.

### Hazards that reach across the level

Sersa Victory and Joris Dormans offer patterns for extending a hazard across time and space, and show them applied to the plants in Bane. Tanglethorn could be passed slowly or hacked away. To develop it further, Dormans randomly picked two patterns, a lingering consequence and a remote solution. The result: cutting tanglethorn angers plants throughout the level, and druidic stones elsewhere can calm them. Moving cautiously costs time, which is valuable in Bane, so the player trades time against risk {{NjIduRLJL7U#15}}. Following the patterns quickly led to networked traps. Spore mushrooms release a lingering poison cloud if a careless step or a nearby fight disturbs them, plants react to each other's destruction, and because all the plants burn, a fire trap or the player's own fireball can create new problems very fast {{NjIduRLJL7U#16}}. Lockwood's principle and Bane's practice match: a trap that hooks into combat, fire and the rest of the level cannot be cancelled by one detection spell.

### Threats that scale with the player

Some of the best combinations in the talks turn the player's own power against them. Caves of Qud's evil twin defect can spawn a clone of the player on entering a new map, with every power and item they currently have. That makes it especially dangerous for strong offensive builds: level up pyrokinesis or find a great rifle, and the twin has it too. In one playthrough the twin also used the player's tonics and gained burrowing claws {{kDKQM27U-Nc#6}}. Because the twin comes from another dimension, its gear vanishes when it dies, so players cannot farm copies of their own loot {{kDKQM27U-Nc#7}}. Jason Grinblat follows the chain further: a generated helm granting the Temporal Fugue mutation, which creates time clones, means your twin arrives with the helm and makes clones of its own {{ClGAApZYIvI#13}}. Qud also has psychic hunters. Accumulate enough mental mutation power and you "make waves in the psychic aether", drawing psychics from across space and time who try to absorb your mind. An earlier "you are being watched" message hints at this, and the sense-psychic mutation shows the hunters as question marks on the map, so you can choose to avoid them or fight {{kDKQM27U-Nc#17}}.

### Magic as a set of hooks into the world

Biskup's plans for Ultimate ADOM are the most ambitious version of interactive magic in the talks. Biskup wants to go beyond detection, hiding and damage spells to highly interactive systems that produce strategies the designers never imagined. The precedents are ADOM 2's "dust powder" demo, in which players laid powder chains, turned things into magma and blew up villages, and Caves of Qud's interacting mutations {{iZUXjbcAfYs#23}}. Biskup describes ADOM's own spells as a little damage here and there, opening and closing, trap removal and curing, and wants flexible effects instead, such as a rare Bladestorm that fills the area with blades that sever body parts {{PTIsUSbQ0TA#10}}.

What makes these effects cheap is the component architecture described earlier in this chapter. An earth elementalist animates a wall into a monster by making the wall entity a being, "with one line" (the prototype demo of this misbehaved) {{iZUXjbcAfYs#21}}. A necromancer grafts a severed orc arm on as a third arm by removing the arm's item component and adding it to the player's body parts, which the body system then interprets {{iZUXjbcAfYs#20}}. Dark magic can turn features and items into monsters with their own stats and capabilities: an animated altar could probably be charmed into a companion, and a Wave of Life animates gauntlets, rations and walls. Biskup suggests combining these, for example animating armour, grafting arms or a dragon head onto it and charming the result {{PTIsUSbQ0TA#13}}. A magical pulse activates crystals of power that send out pulses of their own, so the player can lay a fuse ending at crystals of fire that explode into fireballs. In the demo Biskup clears a room with a planned chain and suggests luring stronger monsters into prepared traps {{PTIsUSbQ0TA#12}}.

Other designers tie magic to things outside the caster. Spencer Egart defines a ritual spell as a repeatable sequence of in-world actions whose effect comes indirectly, through some external system, not from the actions themselves; hitting a goblin five times until it dies is not a ritual {{q4Tf0vf2kiI#0}}. Egart points to NetHack's Elbereth, writing a magic word on the floor to protect yourself, as an existing roguelike ritual, and to speedrun glitch sequences as unintended rituals {{q4Tf0vf2kiI#2}}. Rituals suit long-term effects (enchanting equipment, buffs, healing, protection before a known boss room) rather than combat, since dancing to cast a fireball would get you killed, and they can give magic to classes such as fighters who otherwise lack it {{q4Tf0vf2kiI#8}}. In Q&A they suggested that a flubbed ritual could be detected once the player is clearly attempting one, around 90% complete, and could backfire: a failed protection-from-fire ritual might set the target on fire, which players would then learn to do on purpose {{q4Tf0vf2kiI#10}}. Wildermyth ties its Mystics to the map. They gain abilities by infusing their soul into scenery, so a bookshelf might let them pull knowledge from books into someone's mind or make the wood explode. Since mystics can be a third of the party, each map's scenery changes how combat plays {{vtU3Xa0ShKw#19}}.

### Combinations that cut both ways

Dylan White's Rift Wizard shows how friendly fire and damage types create combinations. A fire elemental ally is immune to fire but not lightning, so a spell like Pyrostatic Pulse, which deals lightning around the edges of its beam, can hurt it. Meanwhile fire stops a troll from regenerating {{VVRomN99Ygw#10}}. Synergies can also snowball in the player's favour. Fire ghosts' fire attacks spawn more fire ghosts, which White says might get out of control "in a way that's very advantageous". White was still nearly killed by a fire-resistant toad {{VVRomN99Ygw#12}}.

Shiren 2's mixer monsters work as both resource and risk. Throw items into one and it combines their attributes, dropping a fused super-item when killed. Harris calls mixers more a resource than a foe, since fusing swords, shields or wands raises how many abilities you can field at once. But every item a mixer eats makes it stronger, so you must be sure you can kill it first {{0MdNwIHwXy0#10}}.

NetHack is the deep catalogue of such interactions. David Craddock uses the cockatrice corpse as an example. Wearing gloves, you can wield it as a petrifying weapon; fall while holding it and you die. Craddock argues that text-based games can support interactions that big-budget games like Uncharted 4 cannot afford, since there every interaction needs a programmer and an animator {{Gs9Nb5ZYRY0#5}}. Adeon's speedrun shows several more. One potion of holy water turns a whole stack of water into holy water, so players dilute unknown potions in a fountain to make it in bulk without caring what the potions were {{rIB0y_kwFuY#9}}. A bag of holding reduces carried weight, but putting a bag of holding or a wand of cancellation inside it destroys the bag and everything in it, a real danger when playing fast {{rIB0y_kwFuY#12}}. Conflict makes every monster fight every other, turning crowded endgame levels into brawls that thin themselves out. It also speeds up hunger, so you only switch it on when you need it, and it makes a pet angel sent by your god attack you immediately {{rIB0y_kwFuY#26}}. Harris notes a smaller interactive feature from the Advanced Rogue family: magic pools where you dip items to bless or curse them, a gamble that mostly catches out new players {{b45orGdFwi0#6}}.

### In practice

- Before adding an element, list the existing systems it touches. Lockwood's test for traps applies to anything: does it connect to combat, monsters, religion, terrain {{ymgmhtkRLyY#7}}?
- Watch for a single counter that cancels an element completely, as detection did to Angband's traps {{LaOcggk7U88#7}}.
- Extend hazards in time and space: give them lingering consequences and solutions that sit elsewhere on the map {{NjIduRLJL7U#15}}.
- Consider threats that copy or respond to the player's power, and close the obvious farm, as Qud does by despawning the twin's gear {{kDKQM27U-Nc#6}} {{kDKQM27U-Nc#7}}.
- Make combinations cut both ways (friendly fire, mixers that grow stronger, bags that explode) so a powerful combination is also a decision {{VVRomN99Ygw#10}} {{0MdNwIHwXy0#10}} {{rIB0y_kwFuY#12}}.

## Resource systems: what every action costs

Every meaningful action in a roguelike spends something: mana, charges, food, time, health, piety, items. The resource system decides how players think about each action. Dylan White gives the clearest framework in the talks; it is built around spellcasting, but its two questions apply to any resource {{kvgKKRLil_8#2}}.

### Two questions: renewability and fungibility

White says two questions describe any spellcasting resource system. How are resources renewed (renewability)? And do different spells draw on the same resource or different ones (fungibility)? Answering them for a game tells you a lot about it, and the answers strongly shape how it feels {{kvgKKRLil_8#2}}.

**Renewability.** The easier resources come back, the more players spend them and interact with the game. That is most rewarding where casting looks and sounds great. White's example is Diablo III, not technically a roguelike, where scarce casting would feel like missing out on the fun part {{kvgKKRLil_8#3}}. Low renewability makes players think hard about every action. That suits strategic, low-graphics roguelikes like Rift Wizard and Dungeon Crawl Stone Soup (DCSS), which trade some audiovisual satisfaction for strategy. White adds that players will always ask for both easier and harder renewal {{kvgKKRLil_8#4}}.

**Fungibility.** With one mana pool for every spell (high fungibility), players spend their time optimising that pool. With separate charges per spell (low fungibility), casting fireball has no bearing on lightning bolt, so players take a wider variety of actions {{kvgKKRLil_8#5}}. The choice is a trade-off. High fungibility gives the strategic depth of picking the best tool for the situation. Low fungibility gives variety, but players are no longer choosing which action to take {{kvgKKRLil_8#6}}.

The framework makes familiar systems easy to compare. A cooldown is a tiny mana bar for each spell: very easy to renew, since it refills by itself, and with zero fungibility, since one spell's cooldown cannot pay for another. White cites World of Warcraft and the roguelike Tales of Maj'Eyal. D&D's memorised spell slots also have zero fungibility, and cards usually have zero fungibility with high renewability through drawing {{kvgKKRLil_8#8}}. DCSS layers two systems. Mana is fungible and comes back after a few turns of walking; piety for god powers renews slowly, through pleasing your god, and buys fewer uses. White calls layering like this a very common trick, because you get the benefits of high and low fungibility in one game {{kvgKKRLil_8#7}}. White's favourite example is DCSS's Trog, whose summon takes a large share of the piety bar and grows stronger with your current piety, so every encounter becomes a choice between pushing through and spending it. By contrast they find Xom, the god with no piety mechanic, the most boring {{kvgKKRLil_8#11}}. NetHack's magic marker is a similar per-use price list. It writes any scroll you have seen onto blank paper, at a charge cost proportional to the scroll's power, so genocide costs far more than magic mapping. In Adeon's speedrun both blank scrolls and charges were the limit {{rIB0y_kwFuY#17}}.

White refuses to name a correct system. Their closing advice is to know what your resource systems are, what behaviour they encourage and how the cost of actions shapes how the game feels {{kvgKKRLil_8#10}}.

### Resources that do double duty

The most interesting resources in the talks carry two meanings at once, so spending them is never a simple choice.

- **Regeneration tied to exploration.** Desktop Dungeons regenerates health and mana only when you reveal unexplored terrain, in amounts that depend on your level and items, and enemies regenerate at the same time. Unexplored space becomes a finite resource and a potential danger at once. Danny Day notes this works because enemies don't attack unless you attack them first {{p48ArjJweSo#1}}.
- **Survival clock and currency.** Caves of Qud makes fresh water both a thirst clock and the world's currency, because it is semi-rare. You can also share it with an NPC in a "water ritual" to gain faction standing and unlock dialogue. Spending reputation can then get creatures to teach skills or share secrets {{owmGknfj6H4#15}}.
- **Strength and influence.** Jongwoo Kim points to Reigns, where each resource is both a faction's material strength and its political influence, so both extremes are fatal: too few troops, or a coup by the general. Kim praises the idea but notes that Reigns' economy is fragile, producing sudden deaths that are hard to avoid without memorising cards. They think that is acceptable there because Reigns embraces whimsical death and builds its meta-progression around kings dying {{oV5PjvcVky8#5}}.
- **Light and weapon.** In Roguelight, which Tyriq Plummer describes, the player has no vision radius and relies on environmental light. A limited supply of flaming arrows both lights the way and kills enemies {{OVSi3NzA0KM#9}}.
- **Use now or later.** Harris describes Shiren-style games as being about resource management and positioning. Items pose a choice between now and later: an herb heals, or, eaten at full health, raises maximum HP. A knockback staff buys room to escape, and a scroll of confusion can disable a whole room {{0MdNwIHwXy0#1}}.

A resource can also be a measure of the characters themselves. Dan Recht describes Darkest Dungeon's stress meter. Too much stress in one crawl causes an immediate affliction, a kind of mental breakdown, and stress built up over time produces permanent quirks, from falling in love to being a bad gambler {{FnJxkId-1eE#5}}. Relief, by camping or at the tavern, costs resources. Recht reads the message as: managing stress is worth paying for, and it is never free {{FnJxkId-1eE#6}}. Gods and shrines work in a similar way in many roguelike platformers. Plummer finds that Spelunky, Rogue Legacy and others let you sacrifice items, creatures or health for a boon, usually to one unseen deity, while Vagante keeps a DCSS-inspired pantheon with distinct personalities {{OVSi3NzA0KM#15}}.

### Resources as pressure over time

Resources can also create pressure that builds over the course of a game. Nic Tringali's The Banished Vault makes logistics physical. A resource gathered on a planet stays there, and moving it takes a ship, fuel, an engine and a navigator. Unlike colony builders, where resources go into an abstract pool, this rule is where the game's hostility comes from, and it spreads the player's goals apart {{nSJA2yVsTFM#6}}. Costs scale non-linearly with distance, so a compact map that fits on one screen still feels vast: a landing costing 10 energy might take three times the fuel of one costing 5 {{nSJA2yVsTFM#3}}. Underneath is near-realistic rocket-equation math, chosen so players can reason about why a destination needing twice the energy needs far more fuel {{nSJA2yVsTFM#5}}.

Jeff Lait names a pattern they call "hidden growth": a cost that rises slowly, cheap to deal with early and expensive later. Players react too late because acting early shows no immediate benefit until the cost overtakes the reward. The real-world example is an invasive species {{LQPGmxytTws#8}}. Lait's game Smith's Hand puts it into play. You are a monopoly blacksmith who can fleece adventurers, but you are also breeding orcs, so you must keep pruning their population. The sweet spot is to farm them for maximum gold without letting them overrun you {{LQPGmxytTws#14}}. Harris describes a Mystery Dungeon variant with a similar outside clock: tough monsters climb up through the dungeon towards the town as you descend, so you build fortresses in the dungeon to stop them {{0MdNwIHwXy0#4}}. The hunger clock is the oldest form of this pressure. Noah Swartz finds the classic NetHack death, trapped in a room going from sated to hungry to fainting, mirrored in The Tombs of Atuan, where Ged is locked in a labyrinth {{IVDN5GDTIvY#2}}.

### In practice

- Name your resource systems, and answer White's two questions for each: how does it renew, and what else draws on it {{kvgKKRLil_8#2}}?
- Decide what behaviour you want. Do you want careful optimisation of one pool, or a wide spread of actions? Do you want frequent spectacle, or every action weighed {{kvgKKRLil_8#5}} {{kvgKKRLil_8#4}}?
- Consider layering a fast fungible resource with a slow, scarce one, as DCSS does with mana and piety {{kvgKKRLil_8#7}}.
- Give a resource a second meaning, such as currency, light, influence or exploration, so spending it is a trade-off, but check that the combined system isn't so fragile it kills players without warning {{owmGknfj6H4#15}} {{oV5PjvcVky8#5}}.
- Make relief cost something, so managing a resource is a decision, not a chore {{FnJxkId-1eE#6}}.
- If a cost grows over time, expect players to act too late, because acting early shows no immediate benefit {{LQPGmxytTws#8}}.

## Prices, trade and simulated economies

Most roguelikes have a shop. Far fewer have an economy, and the talks that look at trade ask first whether the difference matters.

### Does the economy change anything?

Mark Gritter puts the question bluntly: does trade in a game make a difference to the world, or is it decoration? Gritter points at NetHack's shopkeepers and at trade in Elite and Dwarf Fortress, and asks who actually buys what the player sells. What does a merchant do with all the stone tableware a fortress exports, and how does a whole planet end up agricultural {{Q_MFTUYnaf0#0}}? If nobody downstream uses the goods, the shop is just a vending machine with a price list, and a simulation behind it buys you nothing the player can see.

When you do want the economy to matter, Gritter's own generator (Emoji Economy, which builds economies from graph grammars) offers concrete techniques:

- **Give goods more than one use.** A plant can be eaten directly or brewed into beer. That choice gives the economy "play": it has to make decisions instead of everything flowing straight through the production graph to a sink that stands for the population's consumption {{Q_MFTUYnaf0#3}}.
- **Give consumers diminishing marginal utility.** Gritter models utility as a sigmoid that approaches a limit, so the thousandth lemon is worth far less than the first. They note that virtually every real good behaves this way {{Q_MFTUYnaf0#6}}.
- **Decide how trades are found.** Simulated trade has a theoretical problem, how two negotiators agree on a split when economists' models disagree and real people fit none of them, and a practical one, how to find all the good trades efficiently {{Q_MFTUYnaf0#10}}. Emoji Economy runs a round-based auction: a seller region offers goods, other regions bid honestly, and the seller accepts the bid that maximises its utility or rejects them all. It looks realistic but is very slow, because every acceptance means rerunning the optimiser. The cheap alternative, picking random trades and keeping the ones that come out positive, is fast but produces odd results {{Q_MFTUYnaf0#11}}.

You do not need the economy to be efficient. Gritter points out that real economies are not perfect either, and argues that a visibly suboptimal outcome is a feature as long as the player can do something about it {{Q_MFTUYnaf0#8}}. A town short of beer because everyone ate the grain is an opportunity, not a bug.

> I think it's actually cool if players notice that a sub optimal choice has been made if they have the power to do something about it. {{Q_MFTUYnaf0#8}}

### What prices do to the player's head

Robin Mendoza raises the cost on the other side. Fluctuating market parameters can pull a player out of a concrete spatial situation and into abacus work, optimising an objective function rather than reading the board. Mendoza contrasts Go, calling it a primordial territorial auction with no money in it, where thinking stays spatial and about shape; prices are algebraic abstractions, and comparing apples with oranges through them is itself a problem {{ZtXGg6ECwQ8#9}}. Mendoza also asks whether a price system is redundant in a game that already has magic: markets, cyberspace and magic all work as shortcuts that collapse space and time, letting one person act at a distance, so prices on top of magic may be doing the same job twice {{ZtXGg6ECwQ8#10}}.

Mendoza is not against prices, though, and their examples show ways to keep them from collapsing into arithmetic. Michael Brough's 868-HACK charges for programs in two currencies, credits and energy; the double cost makes valuation fuzzier and turns each purchase into a multi-objective choice that is hard to min-max {{ZtXGg6ECwQ8#6}}. In the other direction, a mechanic that fixes an exchange rate between resources, such as Netrunner's Magnum Opus turning one click into two credits, gives players a reference frame for cost-benefit thinking for the whole game, much as the conventional values of chess pieces do. Mendoza suggests designers might include such anchors on purpose {{ZtXGg6ECwQ8#7}}. The two techniques pull in opposite directions: two currencies make valuation harder, a fixed rate makes it easier. Which you want depends on whether the problem in your game is that players optimise too easily or that they cannot tell what anything is worth.

### Trades that are decisions

Not every exchange needs a market. Sersa Victory describes "resource trade" choices, where the player swaps something they have for something useful in another context. ADOM's blacksmiths melt metal items into ingots to repair or improve gear. NetHack's magic lamp never runs out of oil, but rubbing it for a wish turns it into an ordinary lamp that will burn out, so the player decides whether and when to trade permanent light for a wish {{TeURjrAYFEU#10}}. These are prices without a price list: the exchange rate is fixed and the decision is about timing and context.

The goods themselves matter too. Xalavier Nelson, whose market appears in the uncertainty section, chose organs for Space Warlord Organ Trading Simulator because they are easy to systematise: a fixed set of organs with known traits and modifiers such as blood type, rarity and size, which promised a lot of play from a little procedural generation {{SdQxLThAbs8#0}}. A trade system is easiest to build when what is traded already breaks down into a small number of comparable properties.

### In practice

Before building a price system, ask what it adds that the rest of your game does not. It adds decisions when goods have several uses, when values change with how much of something you already hold, when the outcome of trade is visible in the world and the player can act on it, or when an exchange forces a choice about timing {{Q_MFTUYnaf0#3}} {{Q_MFTUYnaf0#6}} {{Q_MFTUYnaf0#8}} {{TeURjrAYFEU#10}}. It only adds arithmetic when the player's job becomes maximising a number detached from the dungeon {{ZtXGg6ECwQ8#9}}. If you keep prices, consider a second currency to resist min-maxing, or a fixed exchange rate to anchor judgement {{ZtXGg6ECwQ8#6}} {{ZtXGg6ECwQ8#7}}, and check whether your magic system already does the work {{ZtXGg6ECwQ8#10}}.

## Characters, relationships and social simulation

Some of the richest systems the talks describe are not about fire or gas but about people: who likes whom, who owes what, and what they do about it.

### From aggregates to individuals

Jongwoo Kim, whose virtue bars for The Shrouded Isle appear in the section on choosing what to simulate, describes another turn that defined the game. Early versions had the player give orders to whole houses, with results calculated in aggregate. It felt wrong, because a cult leader cares about whether particular people behave virtuously or sinfully, and the game needed reasons for the player to want to sacrifice someone. So every action came to involve a specific character, whom the player can order but not directly control; the aim was to humanise resource management with unpredictable characters {{oV5PjvcVky8#6}}. Kim finds the same source of richness in Crusader Kings II: you play the head of a dynasty who needs vassals to like you before they will supply an army, needs marriages with rival kingdoms for the dynasty to survive, and may lose relationships when the ruler dies and the heir inherits. All of it sits on top of fairly straightforward military play {{oV5PjvcVky8#8}}.

Max Kreminski's Star Freighter went through the same shift. It began as a Reigns-like game with three stat bars, one of them a flat 0 to 100 crew morale number. It ended up with a mood for each generated crew member, driven by the player's responses and their relationships with each other, and the individual moods subsumed the average {{66O6WDcT8KY#1}}. The common move is from a pool of numbers to a set of people who each hold their own numbers.

### Small values, scripted-feeling moments

The values involved can be very small. Jim Shepard gives each character a relationship to the player along two axes, liking and respect. An ally who likes you but does not respect you might toss you a potion and leave; a shopkeeper who hates you but respects you might sell you their best gear at four times the price. Events that would normally be scripted around hard-coded NPCs come out of personality flags reacting to game events {{LKCRim02opc#8}}.

Cat Manning shows why it helps to keep such values independent. Dwarf Fortress gives dwarves beliefs, which set what they value, and facets, ranging from -50 to 50, which govern how they behave toward others. Because the two are orthogonal, a dwarf can value friendship highly while being unfriendly, and the clash makes a sad dwarf and a story. Manning, drawing on Emily Short and Tanya Short, notes that we tend to correlate traits that need not be correlated (not every extrovert is loud) and that decoupling them is what creates interesting tension {{q7E1N-fJnrA#4}}.

Relationships also persist past their reasons. In Dustin Freeman's Spirit of the Ages, dire crocodiles expel other creatures from their territory. A gem-collecting troll kept dropping gems there until the crocodile had piled up enough negative concerns to attack any troll on sight {{RYZSdPuvta8#11}}. Nobody wrote a feud between crocodiles and trolls; a grudge simply outlived its cause.

### A social system as gameplay

Caves of Qud makes social simulation something the player does. Its water ritual (water is also the game's currency, as the resource section describes), which the developers call the basic unit of cultural exchange in the game, shifts reputation with the faction of the character you share water with, and also with other factions according to that character's generated backstory. Sharing water with a legendary fish raised reputation with fish by 100, but because this fish's history says he beat the roots at dice, roots liked the player a little less {{owmGknfj6H4#16}}. The same link runs through generated history: a Sultan remembered as a "friend of vines" matters later because the game has more than 60 factions, many of them sentient animals and plants, and some of them cults that worship Sultans {{owmGknfj6H4#9}}. The ritual also creates a rule of conduct. Attacking someone after sharing water with them is a grievous breach of etiquette that makes everyone hate you, and during a live playthrough the developers admitted they had been trying to lure the audience into doing it {{owmGknfj6H4#17}}.

Mark R Johnson's Ultima Ratio Regum treats culture as something the player can perform. Players learn other cultures' dialects and can impersonate them, and together with how they dress, move and what they carry, that can deceive an NPC about where they come from {{qh9vyoMviJI#3}}.

### Relationships that feed back into play

The strongest social systems feed back into the rest of the game. In Fire Emblem: Three Houses, as Nic Junius describes it, using a character more makes them stronger and reveals more of their story, a positive feedback loop that rewards sticking with people; because recruitment and support relationships vary, one player's route can look very different from another's {{sOZH6RPuNJ8#11}}.

Looking further afield, Leigh Alexander reads reality shows such as Love Island as generative systems: contestants cut off from their devices are set on pairing up, and producers inject weekly challenges and revelations, like fan tweets or details from their past, designed to disrupt the bonds they have already formed. Alexander adds the caveat that people are not simply content generation engines and that the format raises ethical issues {{NjYS8XmeaiY#1}}. The design lesson is the producers' move: a social simulation stays alive when something regularly pushes on its existing relationships.

### In practice

- Attach values to individuals, not to groups, wherever the player is meant to care about behaviour {{oV5PjvcVky8#6}} {{66O6WDcT8KY#1}}.
- Use more than one axis per relationship, and keep personality traits decoupled so they can clash {{LKCRim02opc#8}} {{q7E1N-fJnrA#4}}.
- Let relationships change what the player can do: who will fight for you, what the shop charges, which factions open up, what a betrayal costs {{oV5PjvcVky8#8}} {{LKCRim02opc#8}} {{owmGknfj6H4#16}} {{owmGknfj6H4#17}}.
- Plan for disruption, so bonds keep shifting rather than settling {{NjYS8XmeaiY#1}}.

## Emergent stories and making the simulation legible

### Why systemic stories feel true

Rich Wilson calls it the "church of the simulation". A scripted event happens the same way every time and is just someone telling you a tale; an event produced by impartial systems actually happened, in a small reality of its own. That, they argue, is why immersive sims and roguelikes produce more powerful player stories than cinematic games. Wilson's example is a first-edition Dungeons & Dragons wizard who disintegrated a goddess against long odds, a story told for years because the dice, not the dungeon master, made it happen {{wtlLa7BGZcs#0}}.

> If an event occurs because of systems then it actually happened in this sort of microcosmic reality, whereas anything else is just someone telling you about it happening. {{wtlLa7BGZcs#0}}

The talks keep returning to the same kind of anecdote, and they share a structure: several systems, each sensible on its own, chain into an outcome nobody planned. Brian Walker reads a Brogue player's account of being trapped on a bridge by goblins, drinking an unknown potion in hope of levitation, getting incineration instead, falling into water that put out the flames, and watching a burning goblin ignite the swamp gas below {{lfb6uehmrWo#0}}. A Dungeon Crawl Stone Soup player at the 2016 lightning talks recounted a level 17 Demonspawn fighting a snapping turtle over deep water on boots of flying; the kill brought a level-up, the demonic ancestry turned the character's feet into hooves, the boots fell off, and the character drowned {{SDojcmObQzw#13}}. Tarn and Zach Adams tell how Dwarf Fortress cats died drunk: dwarves serving drinks tossed their mugs when given another job, spilling alcohol; cats followed people into taverns; a newly added self-cleaning behaviour made them lick their paws and ingest what was on them; and a default dose of a full mug, set against a cat's body mass, killed them. The only real bug was the dose size. The brothers present it as their whole philosophy, learned from roguelikes, that interlocking mechanisms give rise to this kind of thing {{Zh6dnx9FM1g#15}}.

Jamie Brittain draws a broader point from NetHack's pet cat. Screenwriting's "save the cat" beat becomes, in a roguelike, one option among many: the cat can steal from shops, be eaten or be polymorphed. By offering possibilities a player would never meet in a linear story, roguelikes can change how people think about stories {{C2CzHLtt1Fw#11}}.

### Whose story is it?

Emilia Lazer-Walker pushes back on the canonical example. The drunk cats show colliding systems creating combinatorial complexity, but Lazer-Walker calls it a bad example of emergent storytelling, because it is a story of a simulation running, not a story the player authored {{MNEG1sLSRcM#2}}. Set beside Wilson, the disagreement is about what makes an event matter. For Wilson, the fact that it really happened is enough. For Lazer-Walker, the player needs to have been an actor in it. The Brogue bridge and the Demonspawn's boots satisfy both: the player made a choice, and systems the player did not control finished the story. The drunk cats satisfy only the first. If you want player stories rather than simulation stories, look for chains that start or end with a player action.

### The legibility problem

Emergence is no use if nobody sees it. Jonathan Lessard notes that deep simulations, Dwarf Fortress or Lessard's own stone-age god game modelling the conditions behind the Neolithic revolution, generate far more than players ever see. A typical roguelike view shows only what the player is attending to now, so past events are buried and lost {{D8A9dpITLAU#0}}.

Cat Manning states the design decision directly: procedurally generated traits do not narrativise themselves. In Dwarf Fortress, the strangest stories take work to dig out, and it is often hard to tell which trait caused a behaviour, such as why one dwarf is sadder than the rest. Manning calls this a sliding scale between surfacing causes and leaving players to do the work, and says each game should choose its place on it deliberately {{q7E1N-fJnrA#5}}.

Players will fill gaps themselves, sometimes in ways the designer can use. Stav Hinenzon built Sunshine Shuffle with independent narrative and poker layers, so the same line lands differently after an all-in or a cautious bet. Players project motives onto the poker AI from the story whether or not any connection exists, and coincidences, like a long scripted pause after an awkward line, become comedy {{MyVigc1nDig#7}}. Brenda Romero remembers doing the same with the first Wizardry: the six-character party had no relationship system at all, but in Romero's head they formed rich relationships {{_vf3HO3wn20#0}}. Caves of Qud shows the generative version of this effect. Its independent generators layered together produce results that seem coherent, such as a village of light-shooting mutants who worship a Sultan called the Vermillion Light Bulb, led by a crocodile mayor who explains that his people took up new rituals after meeting the light manipulators {{owmGknfj6H4#11}}. Nobody connected the light-shooting and the light-bulb Sultan; the player does.

Persistence is the other way to surface history. The Adams brothers' high-school game Dragslay already saved every creature that killed the player, and a killer could yell your name back at you, as in Dwarf Fortress {{Zh6dnx9FM1g#5}}. Keeping a record of who did what to whom gives the past somewhere to show up.

### In practice

- Decide where your game sits on Manning's sliding scale, and design the interface for that point rather than drifting into it {{q7E1N-fJnrA#5}}.
- If the simulation generates history, give it a way to reach the player, through records, persistent actors who remember, or the world itself {{D8A9dpITLAU#0}} {{Zh6dnx9FM1g#5}}.
- Juxtapose independent systems and let players connect them; they will often supply the motive {{MyVigc1nDig#7}} {{owmGknfj6H4#11}}.
- Prefer chains where a player choice sets things going, if you want stories players tell as their own {{MNEG1sLSRcM#2}} {{lfb6uehmrWo#0}}.

## Leak or fountain: bugs, exploits and players who break the system

A systemic game will be broken by its players, and several talks argue that some of what they find will be the best thing in it. They differ on what to do next.

### Before you fix a leak, ask if it's a fountain

Jason Grinblat makes the strongest case for keeping rough edges. In systems-driven games, visible rough edges signal that the possibility space is too big and rich to have been filed down, and invite players to explore a messy world together. Grinblat rewrites NetHack's motto, "the DevTeam thinks of everything", as its opposite: the dev team does not think of everything, and the magic is a world more abundant than its makers can know {{kvCky6BbTuE#10}}. The talk opens with the Dwarf Fortress drunk cats, told in full in the previous section, as an example of how such bugs reveal the untamed nature of digital processes {{kvCky6BbTuE#0}}, and closes with the exhortation that gives it its title, following Brian Eno's remark that the sound of a medium pushed past its limits becomes what people cherish about it {{kvCky6BbTuE#15}}.

> The possibility space is too big, too rich for us to have gone in and filed off all the rough edges. {{kvCky6BbTuE#10}}

The main case is Caves of Qud's body swap. If you destroyed your original body in one hit while mind-controlling a creature, you were stranded in the host for good, and the game tended to crash. Players loved it because it let them play creatures otherwise unavailable, and Grinblat saw that its transgression fitted the theme of psychic powers. The team removed the crash and kept the swap as a supported feature, deliberately preserving its air of transgression {{kvCky6BbTuE#11}}. Qud also keeps smaller leaks: a character who dumps strength and takes heavy starting gear can begin too overburdened to move. The fixes are easy (cap gear by carry capacity, set a strength floor), but the team leaves it. A tabletop game master could fold that into the fiction as a character dragging wares exhaustedly to town; a video game cannot, but players can still do it in their heads {{kvCky6BbTuE#8}}.

Grinblat grounds this in a distinction borrowed from Stephanie Boluk and Patrick LeMieux. Tabletop rules are voluntary constraints, a social pact players agree to; video game mechanics are "ontological operations" players cannot step outside of {{kvCky6BbTuE#5}}. A player who finds a leak in the mechanics is not cheating in the tabletop sense. They are exploring what the world actually is.

Several other talks lean the same way. Brenda Romero calls bugs probably the best thing about dynamic systems: Empire of Sin's relationship system produced a narcissist who fell in love with himself and killed anyone else who loved him, and a mother whose children grew jealous and killed each other, "the great Dunbar family feud". Both were fixed before launch {{_vf3HO3wn20#8}}, which shows that admiring a bug and shipping it are separate decisions. Florence Smith Nicholls finds in their research that glitches in procedural systems prompt players to reflect and to share, because they are unexpected and weird, citing a Dwarf Fortress artifact that depicts world history including versions of itself {{MQw3pe-z6JM#4}}. Courtney Liessen suggests twisting players' expectations about genre and franchise rules into soft boundaries rather than hard walls, and notes that games with many interacting systems throw up more surprising balance breaks, including ones the developer never intended and is thrilled to see {{BRJEK20iM7o#9}}. Cat Manning's general tip points the same way: lean into your simulation's weaknesses and design toward them instead of covering them up. Teenagers making foolish choices suits Until Dawn's horror premise; the same behaviour would jar in Mass Effect {{q7E1N-fJnrA#14}}.

### Blaseball: the fountain as policy

Blaseball takes the position furthest. Joel Clark says the team tolerates, sometimes intends, sloppiness: when a system falls flat, they do not fix it but add a new system on top and a link between the two, so the broken one keeps influencing the simulation. "Fixing problems by adding more problems", in Clark's description, adds a lot of fun chaos {{zO05p-e57AQ#7}}. Clark describes the game as made by three authors at once, the simulation, the developers and the community, with the developers always responding to the community {{zO05p-e57AQ#15}}. Alexei Pepers gives the defining example. Fans coordinated to idolise a dead player into the slot for a "steal the 14th most idolised player" event. Instead of closing the edge case, the developers allowed the unintended necromancy and added mechanics so the player came back wrong, killing others with balls. The moment taught fans to go looking for edge cases and system interactions {{H5EU9_Gdu94#10}}.

### Plugging leaks: Crawl and NetHack

Dungeon Crawl Stone Soup sits at the other end. Alan Malloy describes a codified design philosophy that excludes mechanics that are powerful but frustrating, so that optimal play never requires boring grinding. The motto is "when in doubt, do the opposite of NetHack", which they say is not a judgement of NetHack but a mark of how different the games are. Malloy's example is "victory dancing": experience from a kill was applied to the skills used afterwards, so the optimal play was to swing at empty air after every kill. Crawl removed it {{QV57CYrIZow#12}}.

NetHack's history shows a middle path, and how a leak can turn from one into the other over time. Players invented polypiling, piling objects and zapping them with a wand of polymorph. Jesse Collet of the DevTeam says they did not see it coming but felt stopping it would not be fair; instead they added a chance of losing objects in the process and relied on limited wand charges, so the tactic carries risk {{9V_CkJ66uJQ#12}}. Combined with pudding farming, repeatedly splitting and killing puddings to generate junk to polymorph, it became a tedious but potentially endless item engine that could break the game {{KLHJsJuJfYg#3}}. Kawa notes that roughly 42% of winners on public servers never polymorph items at all, which suggests the mechanic is both broken and unnecessary, and that around 2015 puddings were changed so split puddings no longer drop loot {{KLHJsJuJfYg#4}}. Kawa reads that change as a direct response to players abusing mechanics, alongside food rebalancing for vegetarian and vegan conducts {{KLHJsJuJfYg#16}}.

Set side by side, the positions depend on what the leak does to play. Qud's body swap and Blaseball's necromancy add new experiences and fit the game's tone {{kvCky6BbTuE#11}} {{H5EU9_Gdu94#10}}. Victory dancing and pudding farming add only repetition: they make the optimal path tedious {{QV57CYrIZow#12}} {{KLHJsJuJfYg#3}}. Crawl's rule and NetHack's eventual nerf target exactly that case, while NetHack's first response to polypiling, adding risk rather than banning it, kept the cleverness and priced it {{9V_CkJ66uJQ#12}}.

### Edge cases you have to code for anyway

Whatever a team's stance on leaks, it ends up writing special cases. As Nicholas Feinberg recounts, Crawl's Slime Pits treasure vault opens when the Royal Jelly dies, which meant handling unusual ways the jelly can go, such as polymorph, and adding code so the vault does not open if a player dies there, returns in a later game and kills their own ghost {{GMkBT4UbvAs#5}}. NetHack replaces the Amulet of Yendor with a fake if you die carrying it and leave a bones level, so the next player cannot simply loot the win {{HaBp4ETtDKI#3}}. These are not bugs to celebrate or plug; they are the cost of persistence and interaction, and a systemic game should expect to pay it.

### Players exploit; players also constrain themselves

Players find exploits through the rules as written. NetHack's leprechauns steal gold and teleport away, but they do not pick gold up from the floor, so the standard trick is to drop your gold and fight; Adeon keeps most gold in a bag of holding for the same reason {{rIB0y_kwFuY#6}}. In Against the Storm, the self-duplicating Living Matter event avoided buildings, so players fenced it in and farmed its rewards all game {{tWFIQaeSs6M#17}}.

The same players also impose limits on themselves when a system is too open. Kawa describes sandbox games such as Dwarf Fortress as toys that players turn into games through self-imposed constraints, as in Kruggsmash's fort built on ice in the middle of the ocean, which gave a compelling victory condition and a compelling video series {{KLHJsJuJfYg#10}}. The Adams brothers never got far in Hack because they banned Elbereth for themselves, reasoning that a tourist or caveman would not know it, and had a house rule that a wand of fire's last charge had to engrave something funny for the next player of the bones level {{Zh6dnx9FM1g#14}}. Not every leak needs your attention; some players will close it themselves for the sake of the game they want to play.

### In practice: a triage rule of thumb

1. Does it crash, corrupt saves or otherwise cost too much to support? File off that edge, but ask whether the behaviour underneath can stay {{kvCky6BbTuE#11}}.
2. Does it make optimal play tedious, a powerful but boring loop? Remove it or make it pointless {{QV57CYrIZow#12}} {{KLHJsJuJfYg#4}}.
3. Is it clever but too strong? Consider adding risk or a cost before banning it {{9V_CkJ66uJQ#12}}.
4. Does it open new experiences, fit the game's tone, or give players something to share? It may be a fountain; keep it, and consider building on it {{kvCky6BbTuE#11}} {{H5EU9_Gdu94#10}} {{MQw3pe-z6JM#4}}.
5. Is it a harmless oddity players can explain to themselves? Leave it {{kvCky6BbTuE#8}}.

## Keeping emergent systems tunable and under control

The previous section is about leaks worth keeping. This one is about systems that run away, and about building simulations that stay robust when they do.

### Runaway systems

Liza Knipscher's Bioloom, an evolutionary simulation of small creatures, gives the clearest cautionary tale. Mating cost energy and produced one egg. But fruit was free, and a fruit landing on wet ground became a baby clone of the creature that grew it. Under a population cap and hatching spacing rules, the fastest winged creatures spread their fruit furthest, and overnight runs ended with flying triceratopses covered in fruit-bearing frills, purple-grey from colour averaging, plus headless variants that could do nothing but clone {{jBhcHdV5HHo#12}}. Knipscher's lesson is precise: clever workarounds for balance, performance or appearance are acceptable in some parts of an evolutionary sim, but never in its basic energy economy or behaviour, because evolution will find them {{jBhcHdV5HHo#13}}.

> You can do clever workarounds in some places when you're working with this kind of thing, but not in the basic energy economy or behavior. {{jBhcHdV5HHo#13}}

The same problem appears in very different systems. George Moromisato warns that the more complex a simulation, the harder its outcomes are to predict: you get emergent behaviour, but also emergent bugs {{mES03vaZo3U#4}}. Jasmine Otto found that pure forward simulation does not know how to stop; in Otto's prototype, characters who borrowed aspects from each other just accumulated description text forever. The histories were rich, but nothing ended {{NjIDVIIg1lY#4}}. Arvi Teikari says the Noita team fought all through development to make a world that used its dynamic simulation well without feeling too random, because accidents from bugs, AI and material reactions meant anything could happen anywhere, which made gameplay feedback hard {{huMHQGr8PgM#5}}. Jurie Horneman, talking about dynamic content selection, calls this perhaps the bigger takeaway: it is very easy to design a system that sounds good and turns out untunable or uncontrollable, especially when selection cascades into rewards and progression. Linear games were easier to balance; open games where the player can go anywhere are hard to control {{FV_0rmqxbxw#15}}.

The common thread is a loop with no brake: reproduction without cost, accumulation without end, selection feeding reward feeding selection.

### Tools for balancing

Ian Holmes, whose cellular-automaton prototypes appear in the terrain section, points designers at the literature on stochastic processes. Systems that spawn, grow or trigger catastrophes such as fires can be balanced with tools from stochastic physics, chemistry and biology: detailed balance in Markov chains, Metropolis-Hastings, random-walk diffusion models, and models of gases and populations on lattices {{b2VkgcC1xRc#4}}. Holmes singles out the birth-death process as the one to study if you want to stop spawning entities from taking over the whole map {{b2VkgcC1xRc#5}}. Bioloom's cap and spacing rules were exactly this kind of control, and the fruit shortcut shows that a cap alone does not help if one route to birth costs nothing.

On the content side, Jim Shepard treats a list of personality-flag events as a possibility set to prune. Drop events irrelevant to your game, and find a way around events players trigger so often they skew the numbers {{LKCRim02opc#16}}.

### Robustness when the player breaks things

A simulation also has to survive the player. Tarn Adams describes Dwarf Fortress's villain networks, whose architecture is set out earlier in the chapter, as tolerant of disruption: if the player breaks an investigative thread, for example by killing everyone before they can talk, that is fine. The player never knew the thread existed and still solved the murders; the villain keeps working, the simulation keeps going, and the player may meet the network again higher up the chain {{4-7TtPX5uhg#19}}. Mark R Johnson faces the harder version in Ultima Ratio Regum's generated riddles. Required clues can be destroyed, with books burned, NPCs killed or walls broken. Many lead-ins soften this, but if every one is lost Johnson expects to need a higher-level system that tracks how many clues still exist and fabricates a new one that looks as if it was always there {{RDWt6RGN9wM#11}}. The two approaches differ in what a lost thread costs. Where the goal is open-ended, let the simulation carry on; where a specific piece of information is required, track it and be ready to regenerate it.

### Scope a system to what you can deliver

The cheapest control is not to simulate what you cannot handle. Alexei Pepers points to a comment in NetHack's eating code: food can take several turns, but eating a non-food item, as a polymorphed rust monster eating metal, always takes one move, because the DevTeam did not want code for what happens when you eat half a piece of plate mail {{5y_IAdOwaYs#1}}.

Caves of Qud has made similar cuts. Some fun bugs were removed: heat propagation was once aggressive enough to burn down whole mountains and start fires on the world map, and players could power-skate across the world map {{kvCky6BbTuE#16}}. More subtly, the team shrank its powered furniture. Players primed to expect deep systems saw lots of powered furniture and expected something like Minecraft's redstone; a half-sized system implied a base-building game Qud could not deliver. Pepers concludes that once you lean into system suspense, such systems become all or nothing {{H5EU9_Gdu94#15}}.

### In practice

- Every reproducing, spawning or accumulating process needs a cost and a cap, and the cost has to apply to every route, with no free shortcuts in the core economy {{jBhcHdV5HHo#12}} {{jBhcHdV5HHo#13}} {{b2VkgcC1xRc#5}}.
- Give forward simulations a way to end or decay {{NjIDVIIg1lY#4}}.
- Be wary of chains where selection feeds reward feeds progression; prototype them early for tunability {{FV_0rmqxbxw#15}}.
- Prune event sets to the events that matter, and neutralise any the player trips constantly {{LKCRim02opc#16}}.
- When the player destroys something the system relies on, either let the simulation carry on or track the critical pieces and regenerate them {{4-7TtPX5uhg#19}} {{RDWt6RGN9wM#11}}.
- Scope interactions to states you can model completely, and cut systems that promise more depth than you can ship {{5y_IAdOwaYs#1}} {{H5EU9_Gdu94#15}}.

## Systems beyond the single-player dungeon

Most of this chapter assumes one player and one simulation. A handful of talks test what happens to systems with several players, at the table, or in live and physical play, and what comes back.

### Other players as the other characters

Leif Bloomquist set a challenge for their own Commodore 64 roguelike: what if the other characters you meet in the dungeon were other players? That enables shared quests, teaming up against monsters, joint puzzles, and leaving each other tools, clues and equipment, supported by dropped items persisting in the world {{Y0K-e83wYnQ#1}}. Persistence forces mechanics to change. A key teleports back to its origin when used, so that one player cannot lock a door behind them and shut every later player out {{Y0K-e83wYnQ#5}}. Bloomquist designs Dungeon of the Rogue Daemon as a sandbox with no prescribed strategy: set the rules, drop players in, see what happens. At a live demo, about 50 people crowded the first key-and-door puzzle and passed the key along until the door opened, with ten seconds of the talk left {{bpSnPNr6ETc#2}}. Bloomquist wants to add puzzles that need simultaneous actions, one player doing one thing while another does something else to open a portal {{Y0K-e83wYnQ#11}}.

Tyriq Plummer's Clean Sweep builds cooperation into the action system. Any action can be queued to fire with the next player's action. Two players striking an enemy together get an attack bonus, a player pushed in their direction of travel gets a speed boost, and a player standing still with a queued action catches anything thrown at them, which beat dropping items or trading through menus. Plummer recalls being tossed a healing item by a friend while surrounded and near death {{neuJCYmbAG8#13}}.

But the cascading interactions this chapter praises have a cost here. Plummer later limited cascading effects in Scrubbin' Trubble because a burst of simultaneous effects makes everyone stop to work out what happened, which brings play to a halt {{UNBAoc0TJSs#4}}. A chain that makes a great single-player anecdote can be a stall when four people have to parse it at once. If you take a systemic design multiplayer, expect to trade some interaction depth for pace.

### Rules on the table

Board games show other ways to make systems carry forward. In Cole Wehrle's Oath, each site holds cards whose special powers "paint" rules onto that location: a hospital that responds when your army is defeated, secret police. The cards left on surviving sites carry the previous game's rule logic into the next game, so an empire with secret police and a hospital becomes a bureaucratic state whose rules colour what follows {{HgnvMBVKP0M#12}}. The rule set itself becomes generated, persistent content.

David Dunham describes the resolution system Robin Laws designed for Thousand Hells after many failed card systems. It is an exception-based card game whose verbs are adjectives taken from character traits, such as "athletic" and "poetic", so every trait is usable rather than only specialist ones, with dice added for output randomness. Exceptions are implemented by scripts behind the scenes, with bonuses depending on whether the final roll is won or lost {{oipNr8RE5bA#4}}. Moving it to digital added things that are hard with physical cards, such as a card whose difficulty depends on how many rounds it has been in play {{oipNr8RE5bA#5}}.

Yong Zhen Zhou looks at costs in the physical world. Physical properties can replace abstract balancing resources: tear pages out of a real book instead of spending consumables, use real stamina instead of a stamina bar, or make an embarrassing action such as licking a controller, shouting in public or doing a silly dance a kind of "sanity cost" {{KRZHT-gktD0#2}}.

### Live play as a social engine

Dennis Greger, talking about reality TV production as a procedural system, offers techniques that read like a social simulation's game master. Keep social roles in motion: people want a role in the group, even one that opposes it, so assign concrete roles (deciding who eats tonight) and, more effectively, shift relationships, for example by suggesting that someone interested in one participant might also be interested in another {{50u5_hXZkv4#4}}. And run an escalating loop: gather information, use it, make space for participants to act, enable them with an excuse or a plain instruction if they are stuck, then treat what they did as new information. Greger says the spiral can produce utter mayhem {{50u5_hXZkv4#7}}.

### What transfers back

These frontiers carry lessons for ordinary single-player design. Bloomquist's teleporting key is a reminder that persistent worlds need mechanics that cannot be used to deny later play {{Y0K-e83wYnQ#5}}, which applies as much to bones files as to shared dungeons. Plummer's warning about cascades applies whenever the player has to understand an outcome quickly {{UNBAoc0TJSs#4}}. Oath's painted rules suggest that rules, not just maps and items, can be what a run leaves behind {{HgnvMBVKP0M#12}}. Laws' adjective verbs and Dunham's round-dependent difficulty show traits doing mechanical work in a resolution system {{oipNr8RE5bA#4}} {{oipNr8RE5bA#5}}. And Greger's loop, like the Love Island producers in the social section, is a template for a director system that keeps a social simulation from settling: watch what the characters did, then use it to push them somewhere new {{50u5_hXZkv4#7}} {{50u5_hXZkv4#4}}.

## Tensions and open questions

**Few pieces or many?** Eric Billingsley argues that fewer elements force you to tune every interaction and give a more cohesive, learnable game {{3S6uRMCdvNs#19}}. Michael Brough argues that type-specific effects only matter when there are few types {{X4be22WoVgg#7}}, and Dan Norder that added features would dilute Chase's minimalism {{ohH6LBM2EiU#11}}. On the other side, George Moromisato traces NetHack's difficulty and richness to its sheer breadth {{QBZkvtGucBU#4}}, and Casey Yano's team found that unlimited relics playtested better than a three-slot cap {{KYlq0Msu6hc#3}}. Johnathon Pagnutti adds that detailed mechanics may not matter to a given audience at all {{DbZe8rWviS0#5}}. The choice depends on what you want the player to do. Players can learn a small system whole, along with its exceptions. A large one is a space they will spend years mastering. It also depends on who the game is for.

**Consistency or the single exception?** The section on one rule set argues for no special cases {{Zh6dnx9FM1g#12}} {{OVSi3NzA0KM#5}} {{6Whz8bvjAKo#2}}. Brough argues that magic lives in a single exception, as with Rogue's wand of cold not harming the yeti, and that a fully systematic magic system starts to feel like science {{X4be22WoVgg#5}} {{X4be22WoVgg#6}}. The talks do not set the two side by side. One possible reconciliation is the editor's own reading, not a claim from the sources: the consistency argument is about whom the rules apply to (player, monster, object), and Brough's exception is a fact about one piece of content, which every entity can still meet under the same rules.

**Flavour or function?** Brian Walker cut a Brogue joke that broke the food clock {{lfb6uehmrWo#17}}. Brough shows Vancian magic giving up flavour step by step for flexibility until little that was distinctive remained {{zDKheM-E45I#5}}. Ludipe sets the quick reskin against the slower work of deriving systems from a theme {{pYtlU9wGopo#3}} {{pYtlU9wGopo#4}}. The choice depends on how much work the threatened system is doing and on whether the game will still say anything once each trade is made.

**Structured or true randomness?** Aaron A. Reed and Nic Tringali argue for chance that is shaped by, or stands in for, the simulation {{CE7o3GGif30#4}} {{R8DSq8qSJ44@17074#7}}. Xalavier Nelson found that a market made to make sense felt less real than one driven by true randomness {{SdQxLThAbs8#5}}. The uncertainty section suggests that what matters is what players believe about the thing being simulated. That is an interpretation, not a finding from the talks, and it is worth testing with players.

**How much physical simulation pays?** Walker values terrain because it explains itself and supports plans {{lfb6uehmrWo#13}}, and Todd Furmanski wants a chemical simulation for every material {{tmrYfnMrifw#7}}. Arvi Teikari found that unlimited destruction became boring and that most simulated reactions never became decisions {{huMHQGr8PgM#7}} {{huMHQGr8PgM#8}}. Rich Wilson expects finer simulation to move the hard work onto designers {{wtlLa7BGZcs#16}}. Nobody in the talks claims to know how to turn a whole material simulation into play. The open question is how to choose the reactions that earn their cost.

**One pool or many?** Dylan White sets out the trade-off between a single fungible resource, which gives strategic depth, and separate per-spell resources, which give variety, and declines to name a winner {{kvgKKRLil_8#6}}. Layering a fast fungible resource over a slow, scarce one, as DCSS does with mana and piety, is one way to get some of both {{kvgKKRLil_8#7}}.

**Keep the leak or plug it?** Jason Grinblat argues that rough edges show a possibility space too rich to file down, and Caves of Qud kept its body swap {{kvCky6BbTuE#10}} {{kvCky6BbTuE#11}}. Blaseball adds new systems on top of broken ones rather than fixing them {{zO05p-e57AQ#7}}. Dungeon Crawl Stone Soup removes powerful but tedious mechanics on principle {{QV57CYrIZow#12}}. Liza Knipscher allows no clever workarounds in a sim's core energy economy {{jBhcHdV5HHo#13}}, and even Qud cut bugs that burned mountains and a powered-furniture system that implied more than it could deliver {{kvCky6BbTuE#16}} {{H5EU9_Gdu94#15}}. The talks suggest that the answer depends on three things: whether the leak adds new experiences or only repetition, whether it sits in the core economy that everything else rests on, and whether the game's tone welcomes transgression.

**Whose story, and how much to explain?** For Rich Wilson, an event matters because the systems really produced it {{wtlLa7BGZcs#0}}. For Emilia Lazer-Walker, Dwarf Fortress's drunk cats are a story of the simulation and not the player's own {{MNEG1sLSRcM#2}}. Separately, Cat Manning describes a sliding scale between surfacing causes and leaving players to dig them out, and says each game must choose its point on it {{q7E1N-fJnrA#5}}. Jonathan Lessard leaves open how players are to learn what a deep simulation has done out of their sight {{D8A9dpITLAU#0}}.

**Cascades and other players.** This chapter praises chains of effects {{lfb6uehmrWo#0}} {{ziN33iMQVPo#2}}. Tyriq Plummer limited them in a multiplayer game because everyone stopped to work out what had happened {{UNBAoc0TJSs#4}}. How much cascade a game can carry depends on how quickly its players have to understand an outcome.

**Prices: harder or easier to value?** Robin Mendoza praises a second currency for making valuation fuzzy, and also fixed exchange rates for anchoring it {{ZtXGg6ECwQ8#6}} {{ZtXGg6ECwQ8#7}}. They also ask whether a price system is redundant next to magic {{ZtXGg6ECwQ8#10}}. Which you want depends on whether your players optimise too easily or cannot tell what anything is worth.

**Problems the talks leave open.**

- Should a world run only while the game is open, or alongside real time {{TvlZinAvpwg#16}}?
- Can a world be measured without turning it into a score {{TvlZinAvpwg#17}}?
- How should simulated traders agree on a split {{Q_MFTUYnaf0#10}}?
- How do you regenerate a required clue the player has destroyed so that it looks as if it was always there {{RDWt6RGN9wM#11}}?
- What happens to play when the map itself can be reshaped at will {{R8DSq8qSJ44@1660#6}}?
- How should failed rituals be detected, and what should happen when they backfire {{q4Tf0vf2kiI#10}}?
- How do you build puzzles that need several players to act at once {{Y0K-e83wYnQ#11}}?
- Which sources of uncertainty beyond dice and decks could serve a game {{2BHfpAF76J8#6}}?

## Checklist

1. What new problem does the player face in the next ten minutes, and which interaction between systems produced it?
2. Have you listed the combinations you expect players to find as content, next to the monsters and levels?
3. Where does your code ask "is this the player?", and which of those checks could go?
4. Does every placed object carry all its systemic properties, or are some of them props?
5. For each system, what does it reward, what does it imply about the world, and why is it here?
6. How many elements do you have, and does that number suit type-specific effects or systematic rules?
7. Where does uncertainty enter your game, and is each random draw tied to simulated state or knowingly standing in for something you do not simulate?
8. Are interactions written between properties or between pairs, and how much does a new item cost to connect?
9. For each simulated reaction, how will the player see it and what decision does it create?
10. Does each item, trap and hazard connect to other systems, or can one counter cancel it?
11. For each resource, how does it renew, what else draws on it, and what behaviour does that encourage?
12. If a trade or price system exists, does it add decisions or only arithmetic?
13. For the last exploit players found, does it add new experiences or only tedium, and did you decide on purpose?
14. Does every process that spawns, reproduces or accumulates have a cost on every route, a cap and a way to end?
15. When the simulation does something remarkable out of sight, how does the player find out?
