## Music theory basics

[0:02:00] or music theory, but that, as a game developer, you have used quantities to help you express aesthetic phenomena. Music theorists think of scales as the aggregate collection of pitches that occur, either simultaneously or successively, in a passage of music. A scale can be defined abstractly as the series of intervals that separate adjacent pitches in the scale. Most scales repeat that pattern of intervals in all octaves, helping to establish what we sometimes call octave equivalence, where any halving or doubling of a frequency is treated as equivalent. The smallest interval commonly found in Western music is the semitone, which is the distance in pitch between adjacent keys on the piano. MIDI note numbers are expressed in semitones. In addition to note names, we can also assign integers to pitches, usually starting on C with 0, ascending by semitones. These are sometimes called pitch class numbers, and they are equivalent to taking MIDI note numbers and applying a modulo 12 operation. Let’s briefly discuss the diatonic scale, which is the seven-note scale that underpins much of music in the West.

[0:03:01] Perhaps the most familiar version of the diatonic scale is the major mode, which has a pattern of adjacent intervals, in semitones, of (2, 2, 1, 2, 2, 2, 1). There are six other modes of the diatonic scale, which can be thought of as “rotations” of this collection of intervals. We perceive a mode in a piece of music depending on which pitch is emphasized more than others, either by duration, temporal order, loudness, melodic behaviors, or some combination of these characteristics. That pitch is then assigned to the first note of the mode or scale. We’ll come back to scales when we discuss our musical examples.

## Mapping pitch to space

[0:03:35] When composing for games, particularly those that rely on procedural generation, the composer may not know the specific affordances of the game world for musical content, and often the composer does not know the order in which a player will explore the space. For instance, if you generate a dungeon with branching paths or loops that take the player in and out of certain psychological states - again, by encountering monsters, finding treasure, or experiencing architectural or visual changes - you can’t compose a linear piece of music that is guaranteed to underscore the player’s succession of psychological states. Interactive and adaptive audio techniques, such as horizontal resequencing or vertical remixing certainly exist for handling this kind of event structure. Game composers often use audio middleware to build these kinds of possibilities into their music. However, you can also connect musical content with the underlying structure of the game world itself - the coordinate system - as a way to ensure that the musical content shifts in a subtle yet interesting way, in tandem with movement in the world. But how can a composer map the experience of traversing a multidimensional, virtual physical space onto the various dimensions of music?

[0:04:35] For this discussion, let’s limit ourselves to the single dimension of musical pitch, or audible frequency. Luckily, our experience of musical pitch is hardly one dimensional. Most music in the West has a harmonic component - we hear more than one pitch sounding simultaneously, and the sensation of the various intervals between the pitches increases the dimensionality of our experience of music. Furthermore, even in monophonic music, we not only experience the instantaneous frequency of the instrument or voice, but as a melody unfolds in time, we unconsciously compare the intervals between the notes. Thus, even a melody has an intrinsic intervallic component due to our capacity to apprehend recent experiences as a perceptual present, or to compare experiences in memory over longer time spans. There is actually a subdiscipline of Western music theory devoted to creating spatial models out of these intervals, with partially aligned subdisciplines in psychoacoustics and music cognition. This subdiscipline offers us various ways to map a multidimensional coordinate system onto musical pitch. I should note that this kind of theory is very focused on Western music. The idealized listener imagined by these theorists is steeped in this tradition, which includes

[0:05:36] the classical music of Western Europe but also styles that inherited components of its pitch systems, including jazz, rock and pop music, as well as film & game scores. I would gently speculate that spatial cognitive models are formed by experienced listeners of other traditions around the world, but these are not well studied in English sources, and they are certainly not equivalent to what I am discussing here. We should probably be wary of espousing universals, particularly related to the experience of art. Within a spatial model of pitch, you typically place “related” pitches near each other, and “unrelated” pitches far apart. Pitches are usually represented with nodes, and the edges that connect the nodes describe intervals between those pitches. What “related” means depends on the research goals or musical context. For the most part, related means that the pitches sound appropriate when played together, or that they are commonly encountered together, either simultaneously or in near succession. Related doesn’t always mean consonant, although it often does. If you are analyzing or composing music for a survival horror game, you might find that related pitches are quite dissonant when sounding together. There are several types of geometries that you can arrive at when embedding the single

[0:06:38] dimension of frequency into a more intervallically informed, higher-dimensional pitch space. For instance, cognitive scientist Roger Shepard, arrived at a double helix, where the two whole-tone scales are wrapped around a cylinder. Before him, Leonhard Euler and later Hugo Riemann arrived at a two-dimensional lattice, with the musical interval of seven semitones tiling one axis and the interval of four semitones tiling the other axis. In modern versions of this model, from Neo-Riemannian music theory, this model is often skewed, forming a triangular lattice. There is a question about what you hear in music, as a listener, when located at a particular part of one of these spaces. As mentioned before, the spaces represent “relatedness,” and relatedness usually means you are hearing pitches together, either simultaneously or in close succession. Short range relationships might represent harmonies, medium range relationships might represent scales, and the entire space represents the total possibilities of the twelve equal-tempered pitches commonly encountered in Western music. Among the many kinds of spatial configurations available, square or cubic lattices are perhaps the most useful for game designers because they are commensurate with the standard coordinate

[0:07:40] systems used in 2- and 3D modeling and level design, so I’ll limit my discussion to those types of spaces.

## Analysis of game music

[0:07:47] How do these spatial models of pitch help us explain how we perceive music, or potentially how composers can make music? They can allow us to chart the experience of motion in an auditory phenomenon as motion in a metaphoric physical space - and vice versa - they allow composers the ability to underscore movement and exploration in physical space with movement in auditory space. Take for instance the music for Norfair: Ridley's Hideout from Super Metroid (or Magmoor Caverns from Metroid Prime), by Kenji Yamamoto. Although this piece is not from a roguelike, I’d like to use it as an example because it is particularly susceptible to analysis with the spatial methods I’ve described so far. Furthermore, this game has an affinity with roguelikes because of the heightened aesthetic of discovery. Also, many of you are probably familiar with this piece of music. This piece primarily uses Phrygian mode, which is a particular ordering of the seven pitches in the diatonic scale in which the second note of the mode is only a semitone above the first note of the mode. Yamamoto exploits this aspect of this ordering - virtually the entire piece is built around an oscillation between a chord built on the first note of the mode and then a chord built

[0:08:48] on the second note of the mode, so we hear a lurking semitone alternation underpinning the entire piece. However, Yamamoto doesn’t use the same scale the entire time - each time a new section is encountered, he transposes the material up three semitones, and the scale along with it. Moving to a different scale within a piece of music is called modulation. This sequence of modulations underscores the player character’s movement in physical space, as well as ratcheting up psychological tension with each ascent. We can chart this motion using a cubic pitch lattice, in this case one that tiles the horizontal axis with the interval of three semitones, the vertical axis with five semitones, and the depth axis with two semitones. I’ll depict this space with isometric graphs of numbered nodes, where numbers indicate pitches 0 through 11, starting on C. Each cube in this lattice gives us the seven notes of the diatonic scale (notice how one pitch occurs twice). Thus each cube represents fairly well a section of music in this piece. There are some borrowed pitches from outside of each cube - Yamamoto frequently borrows

[0:09:48] an interval four semitones above the first note in the scale, rather than the native three semitones. This is a common trope in Phrygian, particularly when alternating with a chord built on the second note of the scale. Yamamoto moves right along the horizontal axis, each time keeping four common pitches and introducing three new ones with each modulation of three semitones. Moving along this axis allows Yamamoto to use all twelve pitches within four modulations. In contrast to this, moving along the vertical axis - a much more common modulation in Western music - would have taken twelve modulations to do the same. Moving along the vertical axis would be equivalent to moving along the circle-of-fifths or -fourths, an arrangement of the twelve transpositions of the diatonic scale where adjacent transpositions differ by only one pitch. Thus, part of the appeal of this piece is that each new section moves us a surprisingly far amount through tonal space. The aesthetic of surprise and discovery is thus embedded in the tonal framework of the music. Let’s hear a piano rendition of this piece. I’ve also provided a pitch lattice diagram and score with color coded pitches, as well as piano roll notation for people who aren’t comfortable reading music.

## Implementing procedural audio

[0:13:18] With this demonstration we’ve seen pitch relationships in music described spatially. So how can we harness this for a game that uses procedural generation? As mentioned before, Rite of Tephra is a roguelite spelunker. The player navigates an endless series of procedurally generated caverns, finding treasure, unlocking puzzles via gigantic tuning forks, and fighting huge bugs. We make use of the vertical dimension by giving the player a grappling hook. Because this is not a well known game, let’s watch some gameplay to give you some context. Procedural Generation in Rite of Tephra

[0:15:21] The level geometry is generated in several steps: first, we do a weighted random fill of a 3D-grid with cells that represent either solid rock or empty space. We then run a 3D-cellular automaton over that initial random fill to create a cave like structure - the cellular automaton in question is probably best described as a kind of simulated annealing. We prune isolated smaller side caves, and then do a marching cubes pass to generate a coarse mesh. Then we run just a single step of a naive smoothing algorithm on that mesh to round off the square edges, and we use a triplanar shader to give it a natural stone look. All of this is surprisingly efficient. For music purposes, I overlay a cubic lattice of pitch nodes, where the edge length between the nodes is calibrated to the walking pace of the player character. We want the shifting harmony to happen not too fast, nor too slow, assuming a normal pace of exploration. I ended up using 36 meters as the edge length. The pitch nodes nearest the player make up the scalar material heard in the music, and they also control the color of light emitting objects in the level. It should be noted that all of these objects are also procedurally generated thanks to

[0:16:23] Will’s hard work. In our current build of the game, I map the twelve equal-tempered pitch classes onto the standard RGB color wheel, starting with C on red, ascending through the circle-of-fifths while rotating first towards green, then to blue. To assign specific pitches to the nodes, I place the pitch class C, or 0, at the origin. I then tile each dimension with a specific pitch interval. Each new node on that dimension is transposed up from the previous node by that interval. The effect of each dimension is additive, and then we wrap all results with a modulo 12. As mentioned before, the pitch class nodes nearest the player form the scale used in the music. As the player moves, the pool of available pitch classes changes. Currently I fade each pitch class in and out, based on the distance of nodes to the player. We end up with a sphere of influence around the player character. The radius of this sphere is larger than the edges of the lattice, allowing us to “hear” between seven and eight nodes at a time - the perfect amount to make up a typical scale. If we had a larger radius, we would be able to hear eleven nodes, and if it was smaller,

[0:17:23] we’d be limited to one node. Neither of those possibilities are good for assembling ordinary scalar material. The radius ends up being 52 meters, which is about the length of the diagonal of the cubic lattice, and I fully fade-in nodes at 24 meters, using a half-cosine curve to ease the pitches in. This is not a realistic distance function for attenuation of sounds over physical distance, but instead is a musically pleasing way to control what are essentially abstract qualities. There are two scales that allow us to summarize the affordances of a particular tiling on this space, using this sphere of influence: a centered scale, where the player character is sitting directly on a node, and listening to the six nodes connected to it, and an equidistant scale, where the player character is in the center of a cube hearing the eight corners of the cube. All other intermediate positions within the lattice are blends of these two types of positions, or blends between transpositions of themselves. There are many possible tilings to use, but for Rite of Tephra, I selected a subset of tilings that sound appealing to me, and for each level I randomly pick one. ## System realization and demos

[0:18:31] in Rite of Tephra musically? Limited by time and by my desire to use the highest quality orchestral sampler instruments possible, I opted not to do just-in-time composition, but instead, live remixing of pre-composed orchestral stems, each devoted to a single pitch class. The scalar material is controlled by fading in the appropriate stems, and in-game reverb is used to blend the transitions. While this was more expedient than writing a just-in-time composition system, it had its own difficulties. Rhythms would have to be baked into each stem, and any combination of stems would have to work texturally. Furthermore, stepwise melodies became extremely difficult to rely on. Because cave exploration lends itself to an ambient-horror vibe, I was able to deploy a chain of musical events separated by empty space, with events in four categories: drones, morse-code like signals, violent outbursts, and accelerating-decelerating octave leaps. I ordered these categories the same in every stem, and thus for every pitch-class, but the speed at which they occur and where they start in the pattern is different for every stem. Thus, the entire structure is composed of different instances of the same looping pattern

[0:19:32] that phase with each other. As the player explores the physical space of the game world, a subselection of these stems fade in to assemble the appropriate scalar material according to the nearby pitch nodes. Smaller units of pitch information, like chords and melodies, are the emergent property of these systems interacting with each other. In the case of the octave leaps, their interaction is almost like moire patterns in overlapping but non-parallel grids. The entire structure might also be thought of as a mobile or kinetic sculpture, with loops of different lengths rotating in and out of view as the player activates the structure with their movement in the game world.
