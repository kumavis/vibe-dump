## Addressing limitations

[0:15:30] You produce a million by a million texture, and it's locally similar, which means you get repeating output on the scale of your input. And the execution time and energy of this function scales with the size of the input, so you don't want to give it like a 1024x1024 input, unless you have a big set of nodes to run it on. It runs very quickly on small inputs, but exponentially slowly on large outputs. And so, this was a problem for producing interesting maps, even though that input is pretty neat, right? You don't necessarily want to play that map. The second problem took me a little longer to understand, which is how the algorithm will overfit, if you give it a highly detailed input. And what that means is if your input texture, for instance here, has like seven colors, then once it chooses one color, there's really only one other way these tiles overlap. And so, instead of getting interesting combinatorial results from your input, you get just your input repeated over and over again,

[0:16:31] tiled across space. And so, I tried at first to figure out, "Hey, maybe I can build the door locations into WFC, maybe I can build furniture locations into WFC." It turns out that when you do that, you can get output, but it tends to overfit, and the interesting complexity of your results narrows. So, I solved it in a couple simple ways. The solution in Caves of Qud to homogenous output was to do a pre-process pass, where we figure out some large scale structure. In the dungeons I'm gonna show you, this pre-process pass is very simple. It's just picking a couple big shapes, circles, squares, whatever, laying them down on the map, and then filling each of those with a different WFC output.
