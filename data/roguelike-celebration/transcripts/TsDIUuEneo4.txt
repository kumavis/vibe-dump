## Loss functions

[0:02:49] The second part is we need some way to score the output of the generator. So one idea here, I am kind of pulling from the machine learning community, is the concept of a loss function. A loss function takes the output, in this case the output from the generator, and it scores it according to some metrics. Depends on your goals and it could be a number of things. Some really common or really simple metrics that work well for me in loss functions were things like density, or connectedness, or emptiness or jaggedness. They work really well for dungeon generation and stuff like that. We’re gonna calculate some metrics on the output and then we’re gonna compare those metrics to target values. How close are they to the targets we want. A really simple way of doing that is subtracting one from the other and just squaring them. That's pretty straightforward. I want to share two generators and talk about some loss functions that score the output

[0:03:57] of the generators, and then I want to circle around back to optimization. This is some output from a cave generator. The way this generator works is it takes a grid of random numbers and goes cell by cell and it adds up all the values of the neighbor and that becomes the new value in the cell. The magic number or parameter for this is the threshold value. There is one magic number in this generator. We take that new output from the cell and we subtract this threshold value. We get a new number back and pass that through what’s called a sigmoid function, really all that does is just squash everything down into a range of 0 to 1, makes it a little easier to play with. We repeat that process 3-4 times and that becomes the output of the generator. The loss function that I used here was just density. It counted up how many walls and how many open areas were there. The target value was 50%.

[0:04:59] So the generator made things based on those goals. It’s about halfway filled in. It works great. One thing I didn't consider was connectivity. So the generator and the loss function don't really care about that at all and you will see examples that aren't fully connected there.

## Second generator

[0:05:20] This is the output from a second generator that I played around with. The way the generator works is it, again, takes a grid of random inputs, and it goes cell by cell and adds up the neighbors, but instead of giving those neighbors equal weights we let the weights vary. In fact the weights are actually the parameters that go into this generator. Again, at the end, we pass everything through a sigmoid function that just squashes everything between 0-1 and that becomes the output of this generator. This generator has like 36 magic numbers in it. So the generator is paired with a loss function and the loss function here has two goals. One goal is it wants as few corridors as possible. It’s really low-density. But it is paired with another goal. The other goal is we want everything to be as connected as possible. So the output of this generator is kind of, like, tree-based in a way.

[0:06:23] I think that kind of makes sense and that's a really good way to have things as connected as possible but uses as few corridors as possible. It doesn't penalize dead ends, so this generator will produce a lot of dead ends. If I wanted to adjust that I would have to add that as another goal in the loss function, but I wouldn't have to change the generator at all. We’d find new magic numbers that satisfied it.

## Gradient descent

[0:06:48] How do we use the generator and loss function to find those right magic numbers? There’s a really great kind of algorithm that comes from, or it’s really used widely in the deep learning and machine learning community and that's gradient descent. The mental model here is kind of like imagining you are on the side of this really foggy mountain and you’re trying to find your way down the mountain. You know how high you are up on the mountain, so you know your elevation, but you can't see very far. You can see the slope around you but you can use that information to orient yourself and you can walk a little bit, reorient, continue that process until you find your way down the side of the mountain. That’s kind of what gradient descent’s going to do. Let's go back to the generator and the loss function.

## Finding the slope

[0:07:44] The trick here is that we have a generator and we have a loss function. We actually need the slope of that. We need slope information. The question kind of becomes how can we, like, find the slope of a function and one idea you might have is let's go back to math class. Finding the slope of a function is like finding the derivative of it. Can you find the derivative of a computer program and the answer actually turns out to be yes, you can find the derivative of a computer program if it is structured in the right way. That’s a programming technique called differential programming. For my examples, I used Python. There’s a really great Python library called jax which is a drop-in replacement for another

[0:08:44] Python library called numpy. It’s really widely used, it’s a numerical processing library and it’s really great at taking number values and transforming them in certain ways. That works really great in generators and loss functions too. Let's tie it all together. We need to find pick starting values for the magic numbers. A good rule of thumb is just picking really tiny random numbers for the magic numbers. That just kind of is a nice way of getting things started. We also need a batch of random input. We need a batch of these target values. The reason why we use the batch of those is because if you just kind of take one example at a time you’re gonna find a really windy path down that mountain. On the other hand if you take a huge number of examples, it’s gonna take a long time to compute. There is a middle ground there.

[0:09:45] In my examples, I used a batch size of 32. That's kind of where the mini-batch idea comes from. Anyways, we take the parameters, our batches, and we use the derivative of this loss function generator and we find that slope in that local area that we’re interested in we use that information in the same way we’re looking at the slope on the side of the mountain, to nudge the parameters in the right way. We start to converge on magic numbers that tend towards the goals of the loss function. And when we’re converged, when we’re kind of done with that process, we can take those magic numbers, and we can take the generator, and use them in our game or the code that we’re writing, and we can feed in as much random input as you want and we can get the

[0:10:46] artifacts back that satisfy the properties of the loss function. We have kind of flipped the problem on its head. We can change the generator, hopefully without being too scared about messing anything up because we have the loss function that’ll just find really good magic numbers for us. We can also change the loss function, we can change the metrics, or refine it in certain ways, and get new sets of magic numbers for the generator that take into account other properties. We can change other parts of the codebase and feel a little bit more confident we’re not going to break things. The loss functions with those metrics inside of them are really good ways to document the goals we are embedding and that becomes a good way further on in the development cycle when we need to go back and figure out really what our goals were, because they’re kind of crystalized in the last function.
