## A simple premise

[0:02:10] That premise is that you are stealing this machine to try and get away from a horrible situation but your kind is never allowed to ever touch this machine. You have no idea of how it works. You stole it and now you are going to have to put up with this situation and have to push every button, pull every lever and eventually, as you become familiar with the machine, it will fade into a dungeon crawl exploration experience and you will dungeon crawl your way to salvation.

## Procedural world • Voronoi procedurally generated maps with fog of war

[0:02:46] The game is set in a procedural world. It is not presented grid based but it does have fog of war; it is presented from the top inside a radar. It's all generated using Voronoi cells. I make procedural islands I can scale up and down depending on the need during the game. It is displayed inside the radar and you cannot see anything else outside. The descriptions of places you enter are handled through the interface. That is because the story goes the pressure on this planet is so massive that the only thing that can roam around the surface of this planet is these machines called the Nauticrawl. ## Back to the drawing board

[0:08:07] It was scary because we were right in the middle of the project and approaching the final deadline. Clearly, something went wrong along the way because in my mind the actual dungeon crawling part of the game was always meant to be turn-based. It was a slow experience about wonder and discovery. So what happened? What happened I guess is that it became sort of a natural design process to also make the world around you real-time just like the rest of the machine because I didn't want to take players out of the simulated, you know, atmospheric experience. If I was to at some point present the player with a big banner saying it is your turn, or it is the enemy's turn, now you can do this, now you can do that, blocking the player

[0:09:09] during the experience, would have taken him out of the immersion and I didn't want that. The headache was how do I keep not blocking the player while still slowing down this experience in a way that simulates a turn-based dungeon crawling experience. And it turns out, the solution was in the story setting. This game is about a massive crushing atmosphere that forces you inside the only machine that can crawl around this planet. It is all about pressure. What if I use pressure as part of the simulation? What if I let the user pipe in pressure into the engine and I will make sure they take some time to actually build up the required pressure to move forward.

[0:10:10] That will slow down the experience but it will also create some interesting side effects because the player, with some levers, will be able to also speed up the time that it takes to accumulate the pressure to move forward. When the engine is put under stress, it will start giving up, releasing heat and make more noise and the noise and heat will both impact the stealth device which in turn requires more power. Not only did I slow down the experience in a way that was unique but I made the turns part of the machine simulation, which was a side effect I didn't see coming.

## Enemy's turn

[0:11:03] What about the enemies' turn? The solution for that was also part of the story setting. We are talking about a place the game refers to as the "forbidden surface". It has been abandoned for generations. I made it so that naturally both creatures and technology developed this way of not wasting energy when nothing moves, because it's a place that's mostly deserted. I reprogrammed the AI so it only reacts when something makes a noise and moves. At this point, I have the player blocked and being able to experiment with all the different values of the machine and nothing moves until you commit to moving forward. So the game is becoming very strategic and the enemies are all there silently waiting

[0:12:04] for you and the atmosphere is even creepier than before. In my mind it is all working and it is great. What happens when we give it back to the players? They don't realize there are turns at all. They come back to us saying, you know, I make a move and I am still chased by the enemy so I want to make the next move faster and I go fast and this heat coming out of the engine but I am just trying to be as fast as I can because the enemy is chasing me. It is still a big mess. This is not slow at all and I am like damn it. I basically need to teach the player past the initial stage of pressing every button, which is fine if nobody tells you anything. Then there is the dungeon crawling part which has rules and how do I teach the player about rules if there is no tutorial? Yet another headache. ## Teaching players about turns...without a tutorial?

[0:13:10] evil loop of obfuscation. We eventually came up with these ideas of trying to understand when the player is dying repeatedly -- at that point, after you start a run and turn on the machine, the machine enters a safety lock and it will present the player with a check to rebalance the machine. In that check, you might be asked to check on the battery, or the stealth device, or how much hull integrity is left and eventually even the turns. The turns is a bit of a bastard move that the game does on the player in that it will stone the creature next to you when the game starts. You hear the thing roaming around and it is creepy. A radio message will tell you that you have to stay still. The message is broadcasted to all the pilots in the area.

[0:14:10] It is saying stay still, remember that nothing moves until you make a move. Just move very quietly and the creature will not notice you. If you die straight after that, fine, because the game restarts and it will teach you one more game mechanic. It does that one trick at a time. That really worked. It is quite a unique experience. There were many headaches involved.
