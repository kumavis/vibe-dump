## FDM

[0:10:48] It is important to design for failure. In order for this to work and have the satisfying popping off a door to break it down, I needed to make sure it was printable, work and be solid and not too fragile but also fragile in just the right place. The way 3D printers work -- there is a lot of technology for 3D printing. This project is all about FDM, your typical hobbyist 3D printer. This is a high precision glue gun. There are two numbers you need to keep in mind. There is the nozzle width that extrudes the plastic, which determines the X and Y resolution. Then there is your layer height. You have to take these numbers into account. A typical printer is a .4 nozzle diameter so you can print any multiple of .4 you want, but you can't print .5 or it will not work out well. When you have your model, you take it into another program called Slicer that generates paths for the printer. This is a preview of what your printer does.

[0:11:52] This is printing a 2x3 Plarl level. We laid down plastic to have a base, here we lay down plastic to indicate where the exits are, all pretty standard. The interesting part is the next bit. These are the supports for the contents of a room. What I am doing is printing up with as little plastic as I can get away with. I am making sure I am using exactly two nozzle width's thick for the tabs that are going up. And just a little bit across. The other thing that's kind of tricky about 3D printing is you can't print in midair. You lay down a layer, then you lay another on top. The previous layers support the next layer - but you kind of can if you are careful. This is why all the rooms are a centimeter by a centimeter. There is a technique called bridging where you can print in midair which can work if you don't go very far. Every printer has different settings where you can determine how much of that you can

[0:12:54] get away with. All of this is tuned to work this way. The next layer that goes down is - here I am bridging. So those supports that were going up, I go across, and this is only like a centimeter, so you can get away with it. Now I have connected one direction of support. Next, I lay in what supports the iconography. This is standard. Now I need to provide some room. Now I need to provide layers and separation. Next thing is this is bridging again to support the door. You can see there is a big space but there is two lines that are very thin. That gives me something to print on to once again to make the door happen. Once I have done all of that, you kind of get things that pop off in a satisfying and fun way. That's pretty much it. [Applause]
