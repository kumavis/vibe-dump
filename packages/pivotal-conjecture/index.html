    Can you take a trace?

    Conjecture 2.8

    Any fusion category admits a pivotal structure.

    Etingof, Nikshych & Ostrik, On fusion categories, §2.3

    Twenty-one words, no proof, no counterexample, and almost every construction downstream of it quietly assumes it is true. This page starts from the tensor product of two group representations and builds, chapter by chapter, to what that sentence is asking for — and to why it is so stubborn. Every fusion rule, dimension and grading below is computed in your browser from the fusion data itself.

    01

    A number system whose numbers are objects

    You already know an example, and it is worth starting there rather than with the definition. Fix a finite group G — say the symmetries of a triangle, S₃ — and consider its representations: the ways G can act by invertible linear maps on a finite-dimensional complex vector space.

    Three things are true of that collection, and they are the only three that matter here. You can add two representations, by stacking them in a direct sum V ⊕ W. You can multiply them, by taking the tensor product V ⊗ W of the underlying vector spaces and letting G act on both factors at once. And every representation breaks up, uniquely, into a sum of finitely many irreducible ones that cannot be broken further. For S₃ there are exactly three: the trivial one 1, the sign representation ε, and a 2-dimensional one V.

    So the irreducibles behave like a peculiar set of numbers. They multiply, and the product comes back out as a sum of irreducibles with whole-number coefficients:

    ε ⊗ ε = 1 ε ⊗ V = V V ⊗ V = 1 ⊕ ε ⊕ V

    Check the last one by dimensions: 2 × 2 = 1 + 1 + 2. That table of coefficients is called the fusion rules, and it is the skeleton of everything that follows. Write it as a single symbol: Nijk is how many copies of the k-th irreducible sit inside the product of the i-th and the j-th.

    A fusion category is an abstraction of exactly this situation, with the group thrown away. You keep: finitely many irreducible — here called simple — objects; a way to add and a way to multiply them; a unit object 1 that multiplies like the number one; and, crucially, a notion of dual, which is the whole subject of the next chapter. What you do not get to assume is that any of it came from a group. Most fusion categories do not.

    The panel below carries nine of them. Some are representation categories of familiar groups. Some are not, and have simple objects whose dimension is √2, or the golden ratio — which no honest vector space can have, and which is the first sign that you have left ordinary algebra behind.

    Where do those non-integer dimensions come from? Not from counting basis vectors — there is no vector space to count. They come from the fusion rules alone. Treat multiplication by the object X as an integer matrix NX, whose entries are the NXjk. That matrix has non-negative entries, so the Perron–Frobenius theorem hands you a largest eigenvalue with a positive eigenvector, and the same eigenvector works for every X at once. That eigenvalue is the Frobenius–Perron dimension FPdim(X), and the panel computes it by iterating the matrix.

    It behaves like a dimension should — FPdim(X ⊗ Y) = FPdim(X)·FPdim(Y) — and for Rep(G) it is the ordinary dimension of the vector space. The size of the whole category is the sum of the squares,

    FPdim(C) = Σi FPdim(Xi)²

    which for Rep(G) reproduces |G|, by the same identity that says the squares of the irreducible dimensions add up to the order of the group. Hold on to this number. In chapter nine it becomes the sharpest tool anyone has for attacking the conjecture.

    02

    Duality is a wire you can pull straight

    Linear algebra first. Given a finite-dimensional vector space V, the dual V* is the space of linear functionals on it, and there are two completely canonical maps in play. One evaluates a functional on a vector,

    ev : V* ⊗ V → ℂ, φ ⊗ v ↦ φ(v)

    and the other goes the other way, producing something out of nothing:

    coev : ℂ → V ⊗ V*, 1 ↦ Σi ei ⊗ ei

    That second map is less familiar but you have met it: written as a matrix it is the identity matrix, with the sum running over any basis and its dual basis. It is coevaluation, and the fact that it does not depend on which basis you picked is the point.

    Now the identity that ties them together. Start with a vector, tensor in a coevaluation on the right, then evaluate on the left. In symbols that composite is

    V → V ⊗ V* ⊗ V → V

    and if you push a basis vector through it, you get the same basis vector back. The composite is the identity. This is a real theorem about matrices and it is also completely invisible written this way, which is why people draw it instead.

    String diagrams. Draw an object as a vertical wire, read the page from bottom to top, and put an arrow on the wire: up for V, down for V*. Then coevaluation is a wire bending over — a cup — and evaluation is a wire bending back — a cap. The identity above says that a wire with one bend up and one bend down can be pulled taut. It is called the zig-zag identity, and it is the entire content of what "V* is dual to V" means.

    A category in which every object has a dual satisfying that identity is called rigid, and rigidity is part of the definition of a fusion category. The diagrams are not decoration: for these categories the picture is the proof. Two string diagrams that can be deformed into one another without cutting wires denote the same morphism, and that is a theorem, not a convention.

    But notice what the panel's second button did. Bending the wire to the left and bending it to the right are two different moves, and they produce two different duals.

    03

    The left dual, the right dual, and the gap between them

    In ordinary linear algebra there is only one dual because V and V** are the same space — the canonical map v ↦ (φ ↦ φ(v)) identifies them, and does so without any choices. Dualise twice and you are back where you started, for free.

    In a general monoidal category, dualising is a construction, not a space of functionals, and it comes in two handednesses. The left dual X* is the object that lets you bend a wire one way; the right dual *X is the one that lets you bend it the other. Dualising twice on the same side gives X**, and in general there is no reason on earth for X** to equal X.

    For fusion categories, though, the first piece of good news arrives immediately, and it is the opening result of the paper we are reading:

    Proposition 2.1

    In a fusion category, any simple object V is isomorphic to its double dual V**.

    The proof is two lines and worth seeing, because it shows how much of this subject is bookkeeping about which object contains the unit. X* is characterised as the unique simple object whose product with X contains the unit 1. And *X is the unique simple object whose product with X maps onto the unit. In a semisimple category containing and mapping-onto are the same condition, so *X = X*, and applying that twice gives X** ≅ X.

    So at the level of objects, nothing is wrong. Below: the duality map on each catalogue entry, which is always an involution — pairs swap, self-dual objects sit alone.

    Which raises the obvious question: if X ≅ X** for every object, what is left to conjecture? The answer is the difference between an isomorphism and a system of them, and it takes the next two chapters to make that difference bite.

    04

    The loop that will not close

    Here is why anyone cares. Take a linear map f : V → V and ask for its trace. In coordinates it is the sum of the diagonal entries, but there is a coordinate-free way to write it that uses only the cup and the cap: feed in a coevaluation, apply f to one leg, and evaluate.

    tr(f) : ℂ → V ⊗ V* → V ⊗ V* → ℂ

    Diagrammatically, you take the wire carrying f and bend it around into a closed loop. The trace of a map is the map with its ends joined up. Setting f = id gives the dimension of V as the value of a bare circle.

    Now try that in a fusion category. The cup produces X ⊗ X*. To cap it off you need an evaluation, and the only evaluation available to the object X* is the one that eats X** ⊗ X*. So the left leg of your loop arrives labelled X and the socket it has to plug into is labelled X**. The loop does not close.

    By Proposition 2.1 that is a repairable problem: pick an isomorphism a : X → X**, insert it as a bead on the wire, and the loop closes. That composite is the quantum trace, and it is exactly what the paper writes as

    TrV(a) = evV* ∘ (a ⊗ 1V*) ∘ coevV

    Try it in the panel. Then move the slider, which rescales the bead by a non-zero number λ.

    Two things happen at once, and the whole conjecture lives in the gap between them.

    First, the number you called the dimension moved. That is not a bug in the panel. X is simple, so by Schur's lemma the space of maps X → X** is one-dimensional: any two choices of a differ by a scalar, and nothing in the category prefers one over another. The quantum trace of a is a genuine invariant of the pair (X, a), not of X.

    Second, one number on the readout did not move. Rescaling a by λ scales TrV(a) by λ, and it scales the trace of the inverse-transposed map by 1/λ — so their product is immune. The paper calls it the squared norm,

    |V|² = TrV(a) · TrV*((a−1)*)

    and it exists in every fusion category with no choices and no conjectures, which is precisely why the authors could prove hard theorems about it — including that |V|² is always a positive real number, a result that took them most of the paper. Summing the squared norms gives the global dimension dim(C), which is also unconditional.

    the asymmetry in one line

    dim(V) needs a choice and may not exist coherently. |V|² and dim(C) need no choice and always exist. Everything that is hard about the conjecture is the first line; everything the 2005 paper actually proves is the second.

    05

    One choice, made consistently, everywhere at once

    A single object's worth of ambiguity is survivable — pick a bead and move on. What you cannot survive is picking them independently, because then the dimension of a product has no reason to be the product of the dimensions, and a quantity that fails to be multiplicative is not a dimension.

    So you want a whole system of beads, one aX : X → X** for every object X, subject to two demands. It must be natural: compatible with every morphism in the category, so that beads slide along wires past anything else drawn on them. And it must be monoidal: the bead on a product is the product of the beads,

    aX ⊗ Y = aX ⊗ aY

    That is the definition, and it is Definition 2.7 of the paper: a pivotal structure is an isomorphism of tensor functors from the identity functor to the double-dual functor. Unpacked, it is a coherent system of beads. And a category that has one is called pivotal.

    Conjecture 2.8 · Etingof–Nikshych–Ostrik, 2005

    Any fusion category admits a pivotal structure.

    Note what it does not say. It does not ask for a canonical one, or a unique one, or a nice one. It asks whether the set of pivotal structures on an arbitrary fusion category is ever empty. Nobody knows.

    To feel where the difficulty sits, play the panel. Fix a category and a starting system of beads, then try to change it: multiply each aX by a phase λX. Naturality survives anything, but monoidality turns into an arithmetic condition, one equation for every fusion rule:

    λi λj = λk whenever Nijk > 0

    Turn one dial and watch how many equations go red at once. This is what "coherent" costs.

    Be clear about what that game does and does not show. It measures the difference between two pivotal structures, and the difference always has at least one solution — set every λ to 1 and every equation holds. What the conjecture asks is whether there is anything to take the difference of. The game is played on the set of pivotal structures assuming that set is non-empty; nobody has found a way to decide the assumption.

    06

    How many pivotal structures, if any

    The solutions you were just hunting have a name. A system of scalars λ with λiλj = λk for every fusion rule is a tensor automorphism of the identity functor, and those form a group. Two pivotal structures differ by exactly one of them, so the set of pivotal structures on C is either empty or a copy of that group — a torsor, in the jargon: no preferred origin, but a well-defined size.

    And that group is computable from the fusion rules alone, because a λ satisfying those equations is constant on a certain partition of the simple objects. Start with the adjoint subcategory Cad, everything appearing in some X ⊗ X*; any λ must be 1 there, since λXλX* = λ1 = 1 forces it. The remaining objects fall into blocks that multiply like a group, and that group — the finest grading the category admits — is the universal grading group U(C).

    Aut⊗(IdC) ≅ Hom(U(C), ℂ×)

    Rep(G) is graded by the characters of the centre of G; the Ising category is graded by ℤ/2, with the bosons in one block and the spin field σ alone in the other; a category of G-graded vector spaces is graded by G itself. The panel computes the blocks and their multiplication table from N.

    So the counting problem is finished and the existence problem is untouched — a fair summary of the state of the whole subject. We know exactly how many pivotal structures a fusion category has, on the assumption that it has one.

    07

    The fourth dual comes for free

    Here is the result that makes the conjecture look so nearly true, and it is the theorem the conjecture was announced as a strengthening of.

    Theorem 2.6

    In any fusion category, the identity functor is isomorphic to the functor **** as a tensor functor.

    Dualise four times and you return to the identity, coherently, monoidally, with no hypotheses. For representation categories of Hopf algebras this is Radford's formula for the fourth power of the antipode, an identity from 1976; the fusion-category version follows from Nikshych's analogue of it for weak Hopf algebras.

    So the double dual is an operation that squares to the identity. The conjecture asks whether it is the identity — whether that square root can be taken. You are one step away, and there is no general argument for taking it.

    The picture to hold is a twisted ribbon. Thicken each wire into a flat band; then the double dual is what a full 2π turn of the band does to the object at its end. A band with one full twist in it is genuinely not the same as a flat band — you cannot undo it. A band with two full twists can be undone, by passing a loop of it around the end, which is the Dirac belt trick. Drag the panel past 4π and press release.

    The belt trick is an analogy for why 4π is special, not the proof that **** ≅ Id; that is Theorem 2.6, by way of weak Hopf algebras.

    08

    Every fusion category is covered by a pivotal one

    The square root you cannot take inside C, you can always take by stepping outside it. Using the canonical trivialisation g : Id → **** of the previous chapter, build a new category C̃ whose simple objects are pairs: a simple object X of C, together with a chosen isomorphism f : X → X** whose own double dual composes back to g.

    Since f and −f both qualify and are never the same choice, every simple object of C has exactly two lifts. C̃ has twice as many simple objects as C and twice its Frobenius–Perron dimension, and it comes with a forgetful functor back down that just drops the f. And C̃ is pivotal by construction: each object arrived carrying its own bead.

    It is better than pivotal. The paper proves, by pushing the question into the Drinfeld centre and noting that the relevant double is ribbon:

    Proposition 3.34 — sphericity of the pivotalisation

    If dim C ≠ 0 then the canonical pivotal structure of C̃ is in fact a spherical structure.

    Spherical is the stronger condition that dim(V) = dim(V*) for every simple V — the left-handed and right-handed traces agree, so a loop drawn on a sphere gives the same answer whichever way you close it. It is what topological applications actually want.

    This construction — the pivotalisation — is why the conjecture is not urgent in practice. Whatever you were trying to do with a fusion category, you can usually do upstairs in its double cover, pay a factor of two, and proceed. It is also why the conjecture is hard: the cover exists, the functor down exists, and there is no known way to choose a section of it.

    09

    The one good theorem, and its exact reach

    Two dimensions have been following us around. FPdim(C), from chapter one, built out of integer matrices. And dim(C), the sum of squared norms from chapter four, built out of traces. They are different numbers, and the paper proves they are never far apart in one direction:

    dim(C) ≤ FPdim(C) always, with dim(C)/FPdim(C) an algebraic integer

    Call a category pseudo-unitary when equality holds. Then:

    Proposition 8.23

    Any pseudo-unitary fusion category admits a unique pivotal — in fact spherical — structure, with respect to which the categorical dimensions of all simple objects are positive and coincide with their Frobenius–Perron dimensions.

    That is the conjecture, proved, for every category where the two dimensions agree. And a companion result makes it enormously more useful than it first sounds:

    Proposition 8.24

    If FPdim(C) is an integer then C is pseudo-unitary.

    An integer — not integer dimensions object by object. The Tambara–Yamagami category in the catalogue has an object of dimension √3 and is still settled, because 1+1+1+3 = 6. Between them these two propositions dispose of every representation category of a semisimple Hopf or quasi-Hopf algebra, every category of graded vector spaces, everything with integer or even merely rational-summing dimensions, and every unitary category coming from operator algebras.

    So what is left? Categories where dim(C) < FPdim(C) strictly. They are not exotic and they are easy to manufacture: take a perfectly nice category and apply a field automorphism to it. Below is the standard family — the Verlinde categories of SU(2) at level k, and their Galois conjugates, obtained by replacing the root of unity q = eiπ/(k+2) by another primitive one.

    Only the first conjugate sits on the diagonal. All the others have dim(C) strictly smaller — sometimes dramatically so — and Proposition 8.23 has nothing to say about them. The smallest example is the Yang–Lee category, which has the same fusion rules as Fibonacci but assigns its non-trivial object the dimension (1−√5)/2 ≈ −0.618. A negative dimension. Its global dimension is (5−√5)/2 ≈ 1.382 against an FPdim of ≈ 3.618.

    And yet every single one of those conjugates is pivotal — they are braided, in fact modular, and one can write their pivotal structures down. Which is the real state of the art in one sentence: the only general theorem anyone has covers a condition that is sufficient but visibly not necessary, and every category outside it has had to be handled by hand.

    10

    What rests on it, and where it stands

    A conjecture about beads on wires sounds like an internal matter. It is not, because the quantum trace is the gate through which fusion categories reach topology and physics. Close a loop and you get a number; close a knotted loop and you get a knot invariant; glue tetrahedra labelled by simple objects and sum over the labels and you get an invariant of 3-manifolds. Turaev–Viro state sums, string-net models of topological phases, Frobenius–Schur indicators, the modular data of a Drinfeld centre — all of them start by drawing a closed diagram and asking what number it evaluates to.

    The map below separates what a fusion category gives you outright from what it gives you only once a pivotal structure is in hand. Toggle the assumption.

    Where does it actually stand? Open, over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, which is the setting the conjecture was posed in. In the twenty-one years since, the known cases have not moved much past Propositions 8.23 and 8.24 plus case-by-case work, and no counterexample has appeared.

    There is one more reason the question is interesting rather than merely unfinished. Relax the hypotheses and strange things start happening: over fields that are not algebraically closed, simple objects can have division algebras as their endomorphism rings, and Galois theory enters the structure of the category in ways it simply cannot over ℂ. Nobody has turned that into a counterexample over ℂ. But it is a standing reminder that nothing in the argument so far explains why a pivotal structure ought to exist. The evidence is that every category anyone has been able to build has one.

    Which is, in the end, a strange position to be in. Dualising twice leaves every object where it was. Dualising four times leaves the whole category where it was, coherently. The missing statement is the one in between, and it has been missing for two decades.

    Pull the wire taut, and it comes back to you rotated by a turn nobody can prove is trivial.
