I spent a day inside Pōhaku Tumble and I want to tell you what I found before I tell you what to fix,
because what I found is rarer than the mistakes.

You shipped 598 words with a flag on 370 of them that says *I have not confirmed this yet*. Do you know

honest thing on the page, and it is the reason this review exists at all — you asked for it in advance,

And then there are the things you got right that nobody gets right. You have `ʻonaʻona` glossed "dizzy,

the love songs, and they are two different words. You have `akaaka` as "clear, luminous" and not confused
with `ʻakaʻaka`, to laugh. You have `huʻihuʻi` as "cold" and kept it clear of `huihui`, which is a cluster

`ʻauʻau` meaning *to bathe* on another, one ʻokina apart, both right. You wrote `papa ʻaina` — a meal, short
a — and not `ʻāina`, the land, which is the single easiest vowel in Hawaiian to get wrong. Someone will
come along and try to "fix" all five of those. Please don't let them.

Your star compass is correct. All four kūkulu, all seven houses in the right order and mirrored into the

## The ten corrections that matter most

### 1. `makamaka` — "buds, as on a taro corm" → **intimate friend**
This is the one I would fix first. The taro-bud sense is real (PE sense 3). But *makamaka* is one of the
great social words of the language: **"intimate friend with whom one is on terms of receiving and giving
freely; host."** It is the relationship that hoʻokipa is built on. Leaving only the taro buds reads, to a
Hawaiian eye, roughly like glossing *aloha* as "a kind of greeting." Lead with the friend; keep the buds
as the second sense.

### 2. `kōkō` — "to fulfill; to be pregnant" → **the carrying net**
Wrong word. PE's `kōkō` is *"a carrying net, usually made of sennit, as used for hanging calabashes; a
string hammock, as formerly used for carrying the very old."* The fulfil/pregnant word is **`kō`**
(Parker records it under *koko* with the note "Syn: ua kō"). And `kokō` with a short first vowel is a
rooster crowing. Three words, three vowel lengths. The kōkō is also a better object for your board —
*kōkō puʻu*, the fine knotted carrier; *ua kaʻi kōkō*, carried in a hammock because of great age.

### 3. `hākō` — "sugar-cane leaf" → **to carve a path through coral** ❌
`hākō` does not mean sugar-cane leaf in any dictionary I can reach. PE has `hako` (no kahakō) = dignified,
noble; and `hā.kō` = *"to carve out a pathway, as a passage through coral or as a water course."* The
sugar-cane leaf is `lau kō` — which you already have as its own stone. Regloss it or retire it; the
carving-a-channel-through-coral sense would be a beautiful stone on a wayfinding map.

### 4. `oneʻā` and `pālāʻau` — where a missing space changes the word
- `oneʻā` glossed "gunpowder" currently reads **"affliction."** The cinder/gunpowder word is **`one ʻā`**,
  two words.
- `pālāʻau` glossed "wooden fence" currently reads **"to heal with herbs"** — the lāʻau lapaʻau word,
  quoted in PE from a Hiʻiaka prayer. The fence is **`pā lāʻau`**.

Eight more of these are listed in section B of the worksheet: `makawai`/`maka wai`, `pukaihu`/`puka ihu`,
`laukoa`/`lau koa`, `puakala`/`pua kala`, `papakea`/`papa kea`, `ʻaulike`/`ʻau like`, `pīpīwai`/`pīpī wai`,
`hakakau`/`haka kau`.

### 5. `kūnānā` — "to stand and watch" → **puzzled, stumped, at wit's end**
The gloss is the etymology, not the meaning. PE: *"Puzzled, stumped, at wit's end, hesitating, undecided,
bewildered. Lit., stand look."* — *Kūnānā au i kaʻu mea e hana ai*, I don't know what to do. A gorgeous
word for the naʻau field, currently wearing its own literal translation.

Same pattern, three more times: **`panepoʻo`** is not "back of the head" but **"pinnacle, summit,
topmost, most important"** (PE); **`leo paʻa`** is not "one whose voice is stopped" but **a person who
cannot speak**; **`palai maka`** is not simple aversion but **shame** — Andrews puts it with *hilahila*.

### 6. `paʻawaha` — "a bridle" → **a gag**
Andrews and Parker both say bridle, which is where this came from. PE corrects them: *"Gag; to gag the
mouth. Lit., make the mouth solid."* The bridle is `kaula waha`, which you already have.

### 7. `lohelohe` and `mūmū` — reduplication logic vs. attested sense
- `lohelohe` is not "to hear often" but **"to listen carefully; to eavesdrop"** (PE). *Hoʻolohelohe ʻōlelo*,
  to eavesdrop.
- `mūmū` is not "to hum." PE: *"to rinse out the mouth; silent, mum; dull, blunt."* The humming word is
  `mumu`, short vowels. PE's example under mūmū is wonderful: *ʻai mūmū*, to eat silently without smacking
  the lips — said of stingy people eating quietly so no one would know and they wouldn't have to share.

### 8. `hakamoa` → **`hākā moa`** — and a gift for your compass
Your gloss (cockfight / one-leg wrestling) is right — PE's `hākā moa` is a cockfight, and sense 2 is a lua
fighting style fought with feet, legs, shoulders and head, hands not used. But **`Hakamoa` spelled your
way, with a capital, is a CONSTELLATION in PE** — literally "chicken roost." You have a star sitting in
the kanaka field.

### 9. `pana poʻo` — the cultural note that should be on the stone
Your gloss, from Kent, is "to scratch the head to remember." PE adds the thing a dictionary usually
leaves out: *"to tap or snap the head — it was rude or provocative to tap another's head, as the head was
sacred."* The poʻo is the most kapu part of a person. If one stone on the board carries a cultural note,
make it this one.

### 10. Two words that are not words ❌, and one that is barely one ❓
- **`hoakipa`** ("visitor") — not found in any of the thirteen dictionaries. Both roots are real; the
  compound is not attested. For "visitor" the living word is *malihini*. Swap it for an attested
  `hoa-` compound: `hoa hele`, `hoa kaua`, `hoa ʻōlelo`, `hoa hanauna` are all solid.
- **`heiheinalu`** ("to race on surfboards") — also unattested. The word for surfing is **`heʻe nalu`**,
  which belongs on a board like this anyway.
- **`hoalawaiʻa`** ("fellow fisherman") ❓ — attested *only* in Andrews 1836, one line. The formation is
  regular, so it is probably fine, but that is the thinnest evidence on the board.
- Also ❓: **`lāhui kaua`**, likewise Andrews 1836 only.

## The spacing pattern — 106 entries, and it is a date stamp, not a mistake

This is the single biggest pattern in the data and I want to frame it fairly.

Andrews (1865) and Parker (1922) ran compounds together: *halekaua, kiakolu, panipuka, palekaua*.
Pukui–Elbert (1986) separated them: *hale kaua, kia kolu, pani puka, pale kaua*. Modern Hawaiian writing
follows PE. Because you built from Andrews–Parker — which you cite openly — you inherited their
convention, consistently and honestly, across about a hundred words.

The glosses in that list are **fine**. Only the spelling moves. The full list is section C of the
worksheet, and `pe-check.json` carries each one as `spelling`, so the build applies them mechanically. A handful run the other way — PE closes up `lawaiʻamanu`, `manaʻopaʻa`, `hakuʻāina`,
`laukō`, `lauʻulu` — and you have those open.

Two to notice because you already got them right: `lau kō` and `lau ʻulu` must **stay** two words, or
they become "dragnet" and "indistinct speech."

## Cultural flags — the part no dictionary gives

Nothing on this board is desecration. I want that said plainly first. Full detail is section D of the
worksheet; the headlines:

- **Sacred and ceremonial terms now tumbling:** `ʻaumakua`, `ʻai kapu`, `kahu akua`, `pahu kapu`,
  `wai ea`, `papahola`, `pī kai`, `kino wailua`, `hana mana`, `kumupaʻa`. All correctly glossed. I am not
  asking for removals — I am asking for one line somewhere in the colophon acknowledging that some of
  these words belong to religious practice, some of it living, so a turning stone isn't the only frame a
  visitor gets.
- **Genealogy is not a neutral category.** `moʻo aliʻi`, `moʻo kupuna`, `kūʻauhau`, `moʻokūʻauhau`.
  One substantive fix: **`moʻo kanaka` is a tax roll**, not a genealogy — PE: "list or succession of
  commoners, as for taxation."
- **Culturally flat glosses:** `makamaka` (above), **`kuapaʻa`** — glossed as a mollusc, but PE sense 1 is
  *"slave, bond servant; hard labour; oppressed, downtrodden"*, literally a hardened back — and
  **`moʻo lele`**, glossed "dragon," where every PE citation is Bible translation. Moʻo in Hawaiian
  tradition are lizard guardians of fresh water, not European dragons. One line fixes it.
- **1986 English that shouldn't travel to 2026:** PE glosses `leo paʻa` as "deaf-mute, dumb person."
  Please write "a person who cannot speak."
- **Heads-up only:** `puka makani` also means 'anus' in PE; `ʻekeʻeke` sense 1 includes 'scrotum';
  PE records `makapaʻa` with a bad-luck belief I would not put on a game tile; `naunau lepo`, "dirt
  muncher," was an insult thrown at tenant farmers.
- **ʻiʻiwi feathers:** `huluʻiʻiwi` and `ʻahu ʻula` together tell a true and painful story — a threatened
  bird, and about 160 surviving cloaks worldwide. Leave them exactly as they are.

## And one gift back

Your compass is the heart of the piece, so here is something for it.

PE's entry for **`alaula`** — which you already have, glossed "dawn light; sunset glow" — carries the line
***Ke alaula a Kāne***, "the flaming path of Kāne," meaning the eastern sky.

Its exact partner is already on your board too, in the ʻāina field: **`maʻawe ʻula`**, and PE gives
***Ke alanui maʻawe ʻula a Kanaloa***, "the red track pathway of Kanaloa," meaning the western sky.

East and west. Both named as roads. On a wayfinding map made of stones, those two belong facing each
other.
