## How I checked

`wehewehe.org` sits behind a Cloudflare challenge, so I worked through **<https://wehe.hilo.hawaii.edu/>**
(Wehe²wiki², University of Hawaiʻi at Hilo / Ulukau), which serves the same corpus with per-entry
attribution. Each word was queried in up to six forms — as he spells it, without diacritics,
spaced, unspaced, and as `root_a + root_b` — against thirteen dictionaries:

- **Pukui & Elbert, *Hawaiian Dictionary* (1986)** — the gold standard, cited below as **PE**
- **Māmaka Kaiao** (2003+) — **MK**, for modern coinages
- **Andrews (1865)** and **Parker (1922)** — the public-domain dictionaries he already cites
- Andrews (1836), Hitchcock (1887), Judd/Pukui/Stokes (1943), Kent (1986), Wehiwehi Baibala (1872)
- Place Names of Hawaiʻi (1974), Hawaiʻi Place Names (2002), Hawaiian Legal Land-Terms (1995)

Coverage of the 370: **347 found in Pukui–Elbert**, 2 more in Māmaka Kaiao, 19 in older
dictionaries only, **2 not found anywhere**.

## A. The ʻokina and kahakō list

Thirteen entries where the diacritics themselves are wrong. In ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi these are not accents —
the ʻokina is a consonant and the kahakō is phonemic, so each of these is currently a different word
from the one intended.

| his spelling | correct | what changed | why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| `aʻa koko` | **`ʻaʻa koko`** | missing opening ʻokina | ʻaʻa (root, vein) always carries it |
| `hāipu` | **`hāʻipu`** | missing ʻokina | PE's headword |
| `olokaʻa` | **`ʻolokaʻa`** | missing opening ʻokina | PE's headword |
| `pakakahi` | **`pākakahi`** | missing kahakō | PE's headword |
| `ʻōpūhue` | **`ōpūhue`** | ʻokina should not be there | and the root is ōpū 'clump', not ʻōpū 'belly' |
| `kālāʻau` | **`kālaʻau`** | one kahakō too many | PE's headword; MK allows his form as a variant |
| `kūemi` | **`kuemi`** | kahakō should not be there | kūemi with it is MK's modern word for 'recessive' (genetics) |
| `kuilua` | **`kuʻi lua`** | missing ʻokina (+ space) | kuʻi, to pound/punch/add on |
| `ʻahuao` | **`ahu ʻao`** | ʻokina on the wrong root (+ space) | ahu 'mat' + ʻao 'young leaf', not ʻahu 'garment' |
| `hāliʻikuli` | **`haliʻi kuli`** | kahakō drops in the compound (+ space) | free root is hāliʻi; PE's compound is haliʻi kuli |
| `hakamoa` | **`hākā moa`** | two kahakō (+ space) | **Hakamoa** as he spells it is a CONSTELLATION in PE |
| `hōkūao` | **`Hōkūao`** | capital | proper name — Venus as morning star |
| `hōkūloa` | **`Hōkūloa`** | capital | proper name — the same star |

### And five he got right that look wrong — please do not let anyone 'correct' these

These are the hardest minimal pairs in the language and he is on the correct side of every one:

| his stone | his gloss | the near-twin he avoided |
|---|---|---|
| `ʻonaʻona` | dizzy, faint | `onaona` (no ʻokina) = softly fragrant, alluring — the word in half the love songs |
| `akaaka` | clear, bright, luminous | `ʻakaʻaka` (with ʻokina) = to laugh |
| `huʻihuʻi` | cold, chilly | `huihui` (no ʻokina) = cluster, constellation; capitalised, **the Pleiades** |
| `auau` | hasten, move swiftly | `ʻauʻau` (with ʻokina) = to bathe — which he *also* has, correctly, as its own stone |

Also right and worth defending: `papa ʻaina` (meal — short a, **not** ʻāina land), `konākonā` vs
`konakona`, `poʻopaʻa` (the hawkfish, solid) vs `poʻo paʻa` (hardheaded, spaced), and `lau kō` /
`lau ʻulu` kept as two words, which is what keeps them from meaning 'dragnet' and 'indistinct speech'.

## D. Cultural flags — the part no dictionary gives him

Nothing here is a disaster. I want to say that plainly before the list: there is no stone on this
board that I would call desecration, and several of the ones that could have gone wrong are handled
with obvious care. What follows is the layer underneath the definitions.

### D1 · Sacred and ceremonial terms now on tumbling stones

These are all correctly glossed and all genuinely part of the lexicon. I am not asking for them to be
removed. I am asking that the piece acknowledge somewhere — a line in the colophon is enough — that
some of these words belong to religious practice, so that a stone turning over is not the only frame
a visitor gets.

| word | why it is weighted |
|---|---|
| `ʻaumakua` *(in the keep set)* | family and personal gods, deified ancestors who take the form of shark, owl, hawk, moʻo. This is living practice for many families today, not folklore. |
| `ʻai kapu` *(keep)* | the sacred eating restrictions — the system Kaʻahumanu and Keōpūolani broke in 1819. Four words of enormous historical weight. |
| `kahu akua` | the keeper of a god image; a priestly office. |
| `pahu kapu` | the stake marking ground you may not cross. |
| `wai ea` | the small house at a heiau entrance where the ceremonial ʻaha cord was stretched. |
| `papahola` | the level pavement beside a heiau. |
| `pī kai` | purification by sprinkling sea water — performed after a death, and at other thresholds. |
| `kino wailua` | the spirit of the dead; also the corpse. |
| `hana mana` *(keep)* | a miracle, a supernatural deed — PE also gives 'witchcraft'. |
| `kumupaʻa` *(keep)* | PE sense 3 is 'family god, same as the much more common ʻaumakua'. |

**One specific request:** `pana poʻo`. His gloss (from Kent) is 'to scratch the head to remember'.
PE adds what makes it matter: *"to tap or snap the head — it was rude or provocative to tap another's
head, as the head was sacred."* The poʻo is the most kapu part of a person. If one stone on the whole
board gets a cultural note, make it this one: the board is about heads and hands, and this is where
the kapu lives.

### D2 · Genealogy

`moʻo aliʻi`, `moʻo kupuna`, `moʻo kanaka`, `kūʻauhau`, `moʻokūʻauhau` *(keep)*. Genealogy in Hawaiʻi
is not a hobby — moʻokūʻauhau is the basis of rank, land right, and identity, and it is actively
contested in courts and in families now. Two notes:

- `moʻo kanaka` is **not** a genealogy in that sense. PE: "list or succession of commoners, as for
  taxation." It is a tax roll. Glossing it 'genealogy; list of the people' flattens a real social
  distinction between the chiefly line and the counted population.
- `moʻo aliʻi` PE reads literally as "royal lizard" — the same moʻo is the lizard guardian of fresh
  water. That is not a pun; it is the shape of the concept.

### D3 · Mourning, death, and the body

- `poʻo kepa` — hair cut lopsided in mourning. A real practice of grief. Correct, and worth a note
  rather than a bare definition.
- `luapō` (the grave), `kanikau` *(keep — the mourning chant)*, `laweola` (manslaughter),
  `kāne make` (widowed). All correct. Together they give the board a real weight, which is good.
- `puʻu koko` — PE's third sense is the blood-clot form in which the heroes of some traditions are
  born. That is a birth story, not a medical note.

### D4 · Glosses that are technically correct and culturally flat

This is the category I was asked to watch for, and there are three.

1. **`makamaka` — glossed "buds, as on a taro corm."** That is PE sense 3. But makamaka is one of the
   great social words of the language: *"intimate friend with whom one is on terms of receiving and
   giving freely; host."* It is the relationship hoʻokipa is built on. Leaving only the taro buds on
   the stone reads, to a Hawaiian eye, roughly like glossing *aloha* as "a kind of greeting." **Please
   lead with the friend.**
2. **`kuapaʻa` — glossed "hard-backed crab or mollusk."** Correct (PE sense 2, the chiton). But PE
   sense 1 is *"slave, bond servant; hard labour; oppressed, enslaved, downtrodden"* — literally a
   back made hard. Showing only the mollusc hides a heavy word. (The chiton is also used in the
   māwaewae ceremony for a newborn — that is worth keeping too.)
3. **`moʻo lele` — glossed "flying serpent, dragon."** Attested, but every citation PE gives is from
   Bible translation. In Hawaiian tradition moʻo are **not** dragons: they are lizard guardians of
   fresh water, of pools and fishponds, ancestral kiaʻi with names and places. 'Dragon' with no note
   imports a European picture into a Hawaiian word. One line fixes it.

### D5 · English wording that should not travel to 2026

- `leo paʻa` — PE's English is "deaf-mute, dumb person." That is 1986 English. Please render it
  **"a person who cannot speak"** or simply "mute." The Hawaiian is not the problem; the 1986 gloss is.
- `makapaʻa` — PE records that it was considered bad luck to meet a person blind in one eye. True to
  the record, and I would not put it on a tile people flip for fun.
- `puka makani` — PE's further sense of the same phrase is 'anus'. Harmless, but he should know it is
  there before someone else finds it.
- `ʻekeʻeke` — sense 1 of the same word is 'small bag, pocket, purse, scrotum'.
- `naunau` — PE records *naunau lepo*, "dirt muncher," as an insult once thrown at lōpā, tenant farmers.

### D6 · Names that are not common nouns

- `Hakamoa`, `Hōkūao`, `Hōkūloa` are proper names in PE (a constellation and two names for Venus);
  `Mahiʻai` *(keep)* and `Melemele` *(keep)* and `Kaulua`, `Wikiwiki`, `Uliuli`, `Kanikau` are all
  also recorded as **star names**. For a piece with a star compass in the corner, that is a feature,
  not a bug — but they should be capitalised when used that way.
- `Kōʻula` is a valley and stream on Kauaʻi; `Pīpīwai` is the famous stream and trail in Kīpahulu;
  `Kalaupapa` sits inside `laupapa`; `Ala Wai` sits inside `alawai`. Place names carry history —
  Kalaupapa's especially. No action needed, but he should know the board is standing on them.

### D7 · ʻIʻiwi feathers

`huluʻiʻiwi` — the feathers of the ʻiʻiwi, for aliʻi cloaks. Correct. The ʻiʻiwi is now a threatened
bird; `ʻahu ʻula` *(keep)* notes that about 160 cloaks survive worldwide. These two stones together
tell a true and painful story and I would leave them exactly as they are.

### E2 · Root spellings

368 distinct root forms were checked against PE/MK headwords. **Only two** differ:

- **`pōuli` → `pouli`.** PE's headword has no kahakō (PPN *poʻuli*). PE does write `maka.pō.uli` inside

## G. The star compass checks out

I read the compass out of the bundle rather than guessing from the render:

- cardinals `ʻĀkau 0° · Hikina 90° · Hema 180° · Komohana 270°` — correct
- the seven houses, in order out from the equator: `Lā · ʻĀina · Noio · Manu · Nālani · Nā Leo · Haka`

- quadrant names `Koʻolau` NE, `Malanai` SE, `Kona` SW, `Hoʻolua` NW — correct
- every ʻokina and kahakō in those labels is right

That is the PVS / Nainoa Thompson compass, faithfully. Whoever checked it, checked it.

And one gift back, since the compass is the heart of the thing: PE's entry for **`alaula`** gives
*Ke alaula a Kāne* — "the flaming path of Kāne," the eastern sky. Its exact partner is already on his
board in the ʻāina field: **`maʻawe ʻula`**, with PE's *Ke alanui maʻawe ʻula a Kanaloa* — "the red
track pathway of Kanaloa," the western sky. East and west, both named as roads. On a wayfinding map
made of stones, those two belong facing each other.
